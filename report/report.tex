\section*{Scope}

This document is the methods and results write-up for a study of how two modelling choices
change the estimated risk of relaxing outbreak-control interventions:

\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
  \item the \emph{mechanism} that generates heterogeneity in transmission --- whether the
    excess variance attaches to a day, to a transmission event, or to an individual; and
  \item the routine practice of substituting a symptom-onset time series directly for an
    infection time series.
\end{enumerate}

Five models are compared, all driven by the \emph{same} onset-to-onset serial interval so that
the comparison isolates mechanism and anchoring rather than confounding them with a different
assumed interval. The output quantity throughout is the \textbf{risk of additional cases}
(RAC): the posterior probability that at least one further case occurs after a given day, once
the reproduction number is reset to its pre-intervention value. A companion quantity, the
\textbf{risk of additional transmission} (RAT), is reported in a supplementary analysis, where
the gap between the two measures the onset-versus-infection conflation directly.

There is deliberately no extended introduction and no discussion section at this stage. The
sensitivity analyses that the design calls for are listed in
Section~\ref{sec:sensitivity} with their status; none of them has been run.

Every number below is generated from the files in \file{results/} by
\file{scripts/run_report_numbers.py}; none is typed into the prose. The few measurements
quoted from the validation studies are named with the file they come from.

\section{Data and setting}
\label{sec:data}

The observed series is a daily count of Ebola virus disease cases by \emph{symptom-onset date}
for the 2018 \'Equateur Province outbreak in the Democratic Republic of the Congo:
\resultnum{data.totalcases} cases, with onsets on \resultnum{data.onsetdays} distinct days
between \resultnum{data.firstdate} and \resultnum{data.lastonsetdate}, and at most
\resultnum{data.maxdailyonsets} onsets on any one day. The series is padded with zero-count
days out to the withdrawal of the Ebola Response Team (ERT), giving an analysis window of
\resultnum{data.days} days. Those trailing zeros are not padding in the cosmetic sense: they
are the evidence that drives the risk down, and dropping them would leave the estimand
undefined over exactly the period the decision is made in.

Day~\resultnum{data.firstday} is the first observed onset. Day indices and dates are

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lcl}
  \toprule
  & Day index & Date \\
  \midrule
  First onset (index case)      & \resultnum{data.firstday}         & \resultnum{data.firstdate} \\
  ERT arrival                   & \resultnum{data.ertarrivalday}    & \resultnum{data.ertarrivaldate} \\
  Last observed onset           & \resultnum{data.lastonsetday}     & \resultnum{data.lastonsetdate} \\
  ERT withdrawal (window end)   & \resultnum{data.withdrawalday}    & \resultnum{data.withdrawaldate} \\
  \bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent
with \resultnum{data.preertcases} cases before the ERT arrived and
\resultnum{data.postertcases} after. No under-reporting adjustment is made.

\paragraph{The sole-index-case assumption.}
Day~\resultnum{data.firstday} is treated as an initial condition in every model, so the
likelihood runs over days $1,\dots,\resultnum{data.withdrawalday}$. Conditioning on a single
imported case asserts that nothing was transmitting or incubating before
\resultnum{data.firstdate}. For the infection-anchored models this is mild --- the renewal
recursion simply starts from one case. For the \emph{onset-anchored} models it is a stronger
assertion, because it also asserts an \emph{empty incubation pipeline} at day~0: anyone
infected shortly before the index case's onset would contribute onsets in the first weeks that
the model can explain only as transmission from the index case, inflating $\Rpre$. The
onset-anchored models are therefore the ones exposed to a mis-identified index case, and the
initialisation sensitivity analysis of Section~\ref{sec:sensitivity} is aimed at them.

\paragraph{Serial interval.}
Two estimates are available for this outbreak. The \emph{generic} EVD estimate --- discretised
gamma with mean \resultnum{delays.serialinterval.mean}\,d and standard deviation
\resultnum{delays.serialinterval.sd}\,d --- is used throughout, for all five models. The
\emph{outbreak-specific} estimate, mean \resultnum{delays.alternativeserialinterval.mean}\,d
and standard deviation \resultnum{delays.alternativeserialinterval.sd}\,d, fitted to
contact-traced pairs from this outbreak, is retained only for a sensitivity analysis.

The reason for the choice is the variance budget of Section~\ref{sec:delays}. Decomposing an
onset-to-onset interval into an incubation period and a time-from-onset-to-transmission (TOST)
delay requires the two variances to sum to the serial interval's. The generic estimate has
variance \resultnum{delays.serialinterval.variance}\,d$^2$, which comfortably admits standard
published EVD incubation estimates; the outbreak-specific estimate has variance
\resultnum{delays.alternativeserialinterval.variance}\,d$^2$, which is \emph{smaller} than the
incubation variance alone (\resultnum{delays.incubation.variance}\,d$^2$) and is therefore
structurally inadmissible for the onset-anchored models. That incompatibility is itself
informative about how much structure the decomposition imposes, and it is the reason the
outbreak-specific sensitivity analysis can only be run for the naive models.

\section{Delay distributions}
\label{sec:delays}

All continuous delays are gammas, discretised by the method of Cori et al.\ (2013): the mass at
integer lag $s$ is $\int_{s-1}^{s+1}(1-|s-u|)\,g(u)\,\mathrm{d}u$, normalised. Three discrete
delays are used, and they differ in the lag they start at:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{llll}
  \toprule
  Array & Symbol & First lag & Role \\
  \midrule
  Serial interval        & $w_s$        & $1$ & onset of infector $\to$ onset of infectee \\
  Incubation period      & $\finc_a$    & $1$ & infection $\to$ onset \\
  Onset to transmission  & $\ftost_s$   & $0$ & onset of infector $\to$ transmission \\
  \bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent
Write $F_r=\sum_{s=1}^{r}w_s$ with $F_0=0$. The lag-0 rule is what makes the onset-anchored
recursion well ordered: $\finc$ must place no mass at lag~0, so that today's onsets depend only
on strictly earlier expected infections, while $\ftost$ may, so that a case can transmit on its
own onset day. At most one of the two may be supported at lag~0; otherwise infection, onset and
onward transmission could all fall within a single day and the recursion would not be causal.

\paragraph{Decomposition and the variance budget.}
For an infector with onset on day $u$, transmission occurs on day $u+S$ with $S\sim\ftost$ and
the infectee's onset on day $u+S+A$ with $A\sim\finc$. The onset-to-onset interval is therefore
$S+A$, so matching the target serial interval requires
\[
  \mathbb{E}[S]+\mathbb{E}[A] = \resultnum{delays.serialinterval.mean},
  \qquad
  \mathrm{Var}[S]+\mathrm{Var}[A] = \resultnum{delays.serialinterval.variance}.
\]
The incubation period is fixed from a published EVD estimate --- mean
\resultnum{delays.incubation.mean}\,d, SD \resultnum{delays.incubation.sd}\,d (WHO Ebola
Response Team, 2014) --- and $\ftost$ is then the gamma carrying the residual mean and
variance, here mean \resultnum{delays.tost.mean}\,d and SD \resultnum{delays.tost.sd}\,d. A
negative residual variance would make the incubation estimate inadmissible; the implementation
refuses it rather than proceeding.

Discretising and then convolving is not quite the same operation as convolving and then
discretising, so the decomposition is checked numerically: the discrete convolution
$\ftost * \finc$ differs from the discretised target $w$ by
\resultnum{delays.discrepancy} in total variation, against a configured tolerance of
\resultnum{delays.tolerance}. All delays are truncated at lag
\resultnum{delays.maxlag}, which is the analysis window itself, so nothing is truncated within
it.

\paragraph{What the decomposition does and does not pin down.}
The convolution is faithful, but \emph{how} the mean and variance are split between incubation
and TOST is an assumption, and it is not identified by the serial interval alone. Since it is
exactly this split that the onset-anchored models exploit, the sensitivity analysis over
admissible incubation estimates in Section~\ref{sec:sensitivity} bounds how much of the
onset-anchoring result rests on the particular allocation used here.

\section{Models}
\label{sec:models}

Let $C_t$ be the observed count on day $t$. The three \emph{naive} models set $I_t := C_t$ ---
they read onset dates as infection dates, which is the practice under study. The two
\emph{onset-anchored} models set $D_t := C_t$ and treat onsets as onsets. $R_t$ takes the value
$\Rpre$ before the ERT arrives and $\Rpost$ from its arrival onwards, and $k$ is a dispersion
parameter, held at a literature value in two analyses and estimated in the other two.
$\NB(\text{mean}=\mu,\ \text{disp}=\kappa)$ denotes the negative binomial with variance
$\mu+\mu^2/\kappa$, and $\Gam(\alpha,\beta)$ has shape $\alpha$ and rate $\beta$.

\subsection{Infection-anchored (naive) models}

\paragraph{\dlo{} --- day-level overdispersion.}
\begin{equation}
  I_t \sim \NB\!\left(\text{mean}=R_t\textstyle\sum_{s\ge 1} w_s I_{t-s},\ \text{disp}=k\right).
\end{equation}
The dispersion is \emph{constant across days} and does not scale with the force of infection.
That is the defining feature, and the reason the same numerical $k$ means something quite
different here than in \sse{} or \ssi{}. No latent variables; the likelihood is closed-form.

\paragraph{\sse{} --- superspreading events.}
\begin{equation}
  \lambda_t \sim \Gam\!\left(k\Lambda_t,\ k\right),
  \qquad
  I_t \sim \Pois\!\left(R_t\lambda_t\right),
  \qquad
  \Lambda_t = \textstyle\sum_{s\ge 1} w_s I_{t-s}.
\end{equation}
Marginalising $\lambda_t$ gives $I_t\sim\NB(\text{mean}=R_t\Lambda_t,\ \text{disp}=k\Lambda_t)$,
so the dispersion \emph{scales with the force of infection}: each infector independently
contributes $\NB(R_t w_\ell, k w_\ell)$ offspring at lag $\ell$, and these pool by negative
binomial closure. Because the marginal is available in closed form, \sse{} needs no latent
variables --- the only one of the five models with that property.

\paragraph{\ssi{} --- superspreading individuals.}
\begin{equation}
  Y_t \mid I_t \sim \Gam\!\left(kI_t,\ k\right),
  \qquad
  I_t \sim \Pois\!\left(R_t\textstyle\sum_{s\ge 1} w_s Y_{t-s}\right).
\end{equation}
Each infected individual carries a latent infectivity with mean 1 and variance $1/k$; $Y_t$ is
the aggregate infectivity of the day-$t$ cohort, and $I_t=0$ forces $Y_t=0$. Marginally the
offspring distribution is $\NB(R,k)$, the same as \sse{} --- the two models differ only in
mechanism. On this series \ssi{} carries
\resultnum{naivemodelsfixedk.ssi.latents.total} latents.

\subsection{Onset-anchored models}

Transmission is anchored to the infector's own onset through $\ftost$, and $\finc$ maps
infections forward to onsets. This is appropriate for EVD, where pre-symptomatic transmission
is negligible. In the inference-convenient forms below, $E_t$ is the \emph{expected} number of
infections on day $t$; the actual infection counts are marginalised out
(Section~\ref{sec:equivalence}).

All three share the same final step, the map from expected infections to observed onsets,
\begin{equation}
  D_t \sim \Pois\!\left(\textstyle\sum_{a\ge 1}\finc_a E_{t-a}\right),
  \label{eq:onset-step}
\end{equation}
and differ only in how $E_t$ is formed:
\begin{align}
  \text{\coriso}: && E_t &= R_t\textstyle\sum_{s\ge 0}\ftost_s D_{t-s};
  \\[2pt]
  \text{\sseso}: &&
    \tilde\lambda_t &\sim \Gam\!\left(k\textstyle\sum_{s\ge 0}\ftost_s D_{t-s},\ k\right),
    \qquad E_t = R_t\tilde\lambda_t;
  \\[2pt]
  \text{\ssiso}: && Y_t \mid D_t &\sim \Gam\!\left(kD_t,\ k\right),
    \qquad E_t = R_t\textstyle\sum_{s\ge 0}\ftost_s Y_{t-s}.
\end{align}

\coriso{} is the Poisson ($k\to\infty$) limit. It is \emph{not} one of the compared models: it
exists as the target of the collapse checks, alongside its infection-anchored counterpart.

The latent cost is asymmetric. \ssiso{} carries one latent per day with an onset, the same
\resultnum{onsetmodelsfixedk.ssiso.latents.total} as \ssi{}. \sseso{} carries one per day whose
TOST-weighted onset sum is non-zero, and because the index case contributes
$\ftost_t D_0>0$ at every lag out to the truncation, that is \emph{every} inference day:
\resultnum{onsetmodelsfixedk.sseso.latents.total} latents. Section~\ref{sec:inference}
describes how they are handled.

\subsection{The five models, and the switch convention}

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{llll}
  \toprule
  Model & Anchoring & Excess variance acts at the level of & Latents \\
  \midrule
  \dlo{}   & infections (naive) & the day --- NB on aggregate incidence & none \\
  \sse{}   & infections (naive) & the transmission event                & none \\
  \ssi{}   & infections (naive) & the individual                        & $Y_t$ \\
  \sseso{} & symptom onsets     & the transmission event                & $\tilde\lambda_t$ \\
  \ssiso{} & symptom onsets     & the individual                        & $Y_t$ \\
  \bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\paragraph{When does $R$ switch?}
$R$ switches from $\Rpre$ to $\Rpost$ on day~\resultnum{data.ertarrivalday}
(\resultnum{data.ertarrivaldate}) \textbf{in each model's own time index}. In the onset-anchored
models this is unambiguous: $R_t$ multiplies the TOST-weighted force of infection producing
\emph{infections} on day $t$, so the intervention acts on transmission from that date and the
resulting onsets appear an incubation period later. In the naive models $R_t$ multiplies the
force of infection producing \emph{cases appearing} on day $t$, so the switch attributes the
intervention's effect to onsets from that date.

The displacement between the two conventions is the infectee's \textbf{incubation period}, not
the serial interval: a case with onset on day $t$ was infected on day $t-A$ with $A\sim\finc$,
the naive model dates the intervention effect on that transmission to $t$ and the onset-anchored
model to $t-A$. The relevant delay is therefore
$\mathbb{E}[A]=\resultnum{delays.incubation.mean}$\,d. (The serial interval,
\resultnum{delays.serialinterval.mean}\,d, is the infector-onset to infectee-onset gap and does
not separate the conventions.)

This asymmetry is deliberate and is a reportable consequence of onset-as-infection conflation
rather than a modelling artefact: it is one of the mechanisms by which naive models mis-date an
intervention's effect, and Section~\ref{sec:results-onset} quantifies what it does to $\Rpost$
and to the RAC curve. Section~\ref{sec:sensitivity} lists the shifted-switch run that would
separate this indexing effect from the structural one.

\section{The two forms of the onset-anchored models are equivalent}
\label{sec:equivalence}

The ``natural'' definitions of the onset-anchored models generate latent infection counts $J_t$
and map them forward to onsets; the inference-convenient forms of Section~\ref{sec:models} work
directly with onsets. They define the same joint law for the observed onset process $(D_t)$.

In the natural form, with $\lambda_t=\sum_{s\ge0}\ftost_s D_{t-s}$ (\coriso{}, \sseso{}) or
$\lambda_t=\sum_{s\ge0}\ftost_s Y_{t-s}$ (\ssiso{}):
(1) infections $J_t$ are drawn from the model's offspring distribution with mean $R_t\lambda_t$;
(2) the $J_u$ infections of day $u$ are allocated to onset days by independent incubation draws,
$(N_{u,a})_{a\ge1}\mid J_u \sim \mathrm{Multinomial}(J_u, (\finc_a))$;
(3) onsets are $D_t=\sum_{a\ge1}N_{t-a,a}$.

\subsection{The Poisson case (\coriso{})}

Let $E_t=R_t\lambda_t$, so $J_t\mid E_t\sim\Pois(E_t)$.

\emph{Step 1 --- Poisson thinning.} Multinomial splitting of a Poisson count yields
\emph{independent} Poisson parts: conditional on $E_u$,
$N_{u,a}\sim\Pois(E_u\finc_a)$ independently over $a\ge1$, with $J_u=\sum_a N_{u,a}$ recovered
as their sum. The latent $J_u$ therefore carries no information beyond the parts and can be
dropped.

\emph{Step 2 --- Poisson superposition.} $D_t=\sum_{a\ge1}N_{t-a,a}$ is a sum of independent
Poissons, hence
\begin{equation}
  D_t \mid (E_u)_{u<t} \sim \Pois\!\left(\textstyle\sum_{a\ge1}\finc_a E_{t-a}\right),
\end{equation}
which is exactly the inference-convenient form.

\emph{Step 3 --- the joint law, not just the marginal.} Because $\finc$ has no mass at lag~0,
$E_{t-a}$ for $a\ge1$ is a function of $D_{0:t-1}$ only, so the display above is precisely the
conditional law $p(D_t\mid D_{0:t-1})$. The parts $\{N_{u,a}: u+a=t\}$ composing $D_t$ are
disjoint from those composing any other $D_{t'}$, so distinct days' onsets are conditionally
independent given the $E$s. Chaining $p(D_{0:T})=\prod_t p(D_t\mid D_{0:t-1})$ gives equality of
the full joint laws. \hfill$\square$

\subsection{\sseso{}}

\emph{Step A --- Poisson--Gamma mixture.} If $\tilde\lambda_t\sim\Gam(k\lambda_t,k)$ and
$J_t\mid\tilde\lambda_t\sim\Pois(R_t\tilde\lambda_t)$, then
$R_t\tilde\lambda_t\sim\Gam(k\lambda_t,\ k/R_t)$ and, marginally,
\begin{equation}
  J_t \sim \NB\!\left(\text{mean}=R_t\lambda_t,\ \text{disp}=k\lambda_t\right),
\end{equation}
which is the natural-form offspring law. The convenient form merely \emph{un-marginalises} the
negative binomial into an explicit per-day Gamma random effect.

\emph{Step B.} Conditional on $\tilde\lambda_t$ (equivalently on $E_t=R_t\tilde\lambda_t$) the
infection counts are Poisson, so Steps 1--3 above apply verbatim. The $\tilde\lambda_t$ are
independent across $t$ given $(\lambda_t)$, and each $\lambda_t$ is a function of $D_{\le t}$, so
the day-$t$ conditional still depends only on $D_{0:t-1}$ once $\tilde\lambda_{<t}$ is
integrated out. \hfill$\square$

\emph{Consequence.} The observed onsets are \textbf{not} marginally negative binomial: $D_t$
pools contributions from many days, each carrying its own independent Gamma. There is no
closed-form likelihood and the $\tilde\lambda_t$ must be sampled --- in contrast to the
infection-anchored \sse{}, which is latent-free.

\subsection{\ssiso{}}

Identical to the Poisson case with $E_t=R_t\sum_{s\ge0}\ftost_s Y_{t-s}$. The Gamma
infectivities are already explicit in both forms, and the thinning and superposition arguments
do not touch them. \hfill$\square$

\subsection{What is tested}

The equivalence is encoded as tests rather than left as an argument: setting $\finc_1=1$
(deterministic one-day incubation) and shifting indices recovers the infection-anchored models
with $w_s=\ftost_{s-1}$; $k\to\infty$ collapses \sseso{} and \ssiso{} onto \coriso{};
forward-simulating the natural form (explicit $J_t$ plus a multinomial split) and the convenient
form gives matching empirical distributions of $D_{1:T}$; and the convenient-form likelihood
matches a brute-force marginalisation over small $J_t$ on short synthetic histories.

\section{The risk of additional cases and the risk of additional transmission}
\label{sec:rac}

\subsection{RAC is a retrospective reset posterior predictive}

Writing the estimand as $P(\cdot\mid\text{data up to day }t)$ invites a filtering reading that
the analysis does not use. It is stated instead as a procedure:

\begin{quote}
Fit $(\Rpre,\Rpost,k)$ \emph{and all latent states} to the complete outbreak record, days
$0$--$\resultnum{data.withdrawalday}$. Then, for each day $t$: retain the inferred state
attached to the observed history through day $t$, discard the realised trajectory after day
$t$, reset $R$ to $\Rpre$ from day $t+1$ onwards, and simulate a counterfactual future.
\textbf{RAC$(t)$ is the posterior probability that this replicated future contains at least one
further case.}
\end{quote}

Two things follow that the shorthand hides. The fit is fully retrospective by design: the
parameters come from all \resultnum{data.days} days, not from the history through $t$. And for
the latent-variable models the \emph{retained state} also comes from the full-data posterior, so
it is a \emph{smoothed} state informed by data after day $t$ --- see Section~\ref{sec:smoothing}.

RAC$(t)$ is one number per day, not a distribution: it is a posterior probability, so the
average over draws happens inside it, and what is reported is
$1-\overline{\exp(\log P(\text{no further case}))}$ over the posterior draws. Because it is an
average it carries Monte-Carlo error, which is computed from the between-chain spread and
written beside every curve; no curve is published without it.

\subsection{Closed forms for the naive models}

Define the pooled remaining-transmission weight
\begin{equation}
  \Lambda(t) = \sum_{u\le t} I_u\,(1-F_{t-u})
  \quad(\sse{},\ \dlo{}),
  \qquad
  \Lambda_Y(t) = \sum_{u\le t} Y_u\,(1-F_{t-u})
  \quad(\ssi{}),
\end{equation}
where $(1-F_{t-u})$ is the probability that a case first appearing on day $u$ still has
transmission to come after day $t$; the $u=t$ term enters with full weight. Then

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{ll}
  \toprule
  Model & $P(\text{no further cases after day } t)$ \\
  \midrule
  \sse{} & $\left(1+\Rpre/k\right)^{-k\Lambda(t)}$ \\
  \ssi{} & $\mathbb{E}_{\text{posterior}}\!\left[\exp\!\left(-\Rpre\Lambda_Y(t)\right)\right]$ \\
  \dlo{} & $\prod_{j>t}\left(1+\Rpre\mu_j/k\right)^{-k}$,\quad
           $\mu_j=\sum_{s\ge1}w_s I_{j-s}$ with $I_u:=0$ for $u>t$ \\
  \bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent
The \dlo{} product is finite in practice, since $\mu_j=0$ once $j$ exceeds $t$ plus the support
of $w$.

\subsection{Why \dlo{} is structurally different}
\label{sec:dlo-structure}

\sse{} and \ssi{} depend on the observed history \emph{only through the scalar} $\Lambda(t)$:
the total remaining offspring pools into a single $\NB(\Rpre\Lambda,\ k\Lambda)$ or, given the
latents, $\Pois(\Rpre\Lambda_Y)$. \dlo{} does not. Its risk depends on the whole temporal
\emph{profile} $(\mu_j)_{j>t}$ of the future force of infection, because a fresh dispersion $k$
that does not scale with the force of infection is applied on each future day. Writing
$\varphi(\mu)=-k\log(1+R\mu/k)$, which is convex with $\varphi(0)=0$ and hence superadditive,
\begin{equation}
  -R\Lambda(t) \;\le\; \log P(\text{no further cases})_{\dlo{}} = \sum_{j>t}\varphi(\mu_j)
  \;\le\; \varphi\big(\Lambda(t)\big).
  \label{eq:dlo-bounds}
\end{equation}
Spreading the same total $\Lambda(t)$ across days \emph{dilutes} the overdispersion, so a value
of $k$ calibrated as an individual-level offspring dispersion does far less work in \dlo{} than
in \sse{} or \ssi{}.

The bounds in \eqref{eq:dlo-bounds} hold always; the \emph{direction} of the comparison with
\sse{} does not, and is a property of this outbreak's thin, spread-out future force of
infection rather than a theorem. Concentrating $\Lambda(t)$ into a day or two puts \dlo{} below
\sse{} instead. The implementation and its tests are written against the bounds.

\subsection{The onset-anchored reset state, and RAT}
\label{sec:pipeline}

Under the onset-anchored models the observed onsets do not determine the state needed to project
forward. At day $t$ there is a latent \textbf{incubation pipeline} of individuals already
infected but not yet symptomatic, of size
\begin{equation}
  \Pois\!\left(\sum_{u\le t} E_u\Big(1-\sum_{a\le t-u}\finc_a\Big)\right)
\end{equation}
given the $E$s, allocated to future onset days by the residual incubation distribution
$\finc_a/(1-\sum_{a'\le t-u}\finc_{a'})$ for $a>t-u$. The implementation constructs this
explicitly. Restarting the recursion from the observed onsets alone would silently drop the
pipeline and understate the risk; this is an implementation requirement of the estimand, not an
ambiguity in it.

With $H(t)$ the contribution of future transmission from retained onset cohorts, $M(t)$ the mean
size of the retained pipeline and $c$ the expected onward transmission a pipeline individual
generates, the conditional zero-probabilities are evaluated analytically as
\begin{align}
  \log P(\text{no further case}) &= -H(t)-M(t), \\
  \log P(\text{no further transmission}) &= -H(t)-M(t)\big(1-e^{-c}\big),
\end{align}
and averaged over the posterior draws. Hence:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{RAC}$(t)$ --- the probability of at least one further \emph{case} in the onset
    series after day $t$. It includes pipeline individuals who become symptomatic after $t$ even
    if no new transmission occurs, so it does not fall towards zero until the pipeline has
    drained. This is the directly observable quantity and the headline curve everywhere.
  \item \textbf{RAT}$(t)$ --- the probability of at least one further \emph{transmission event}
    after day $t$. It isolates the effect of relaxing interventions but is not directly
    observable.
\end{itemize}

\noindent
$\text{RAC}(t)\ge\text{RAT}(t)$ always, and the gap is exactly the pipeline contribution. Under
the three naive models the two quantities coincide \emph{by assumption} --- which is precisely
the conflation this study is about --- so RAT is reported only in the supplementary analysis of
Section~\ref{sec:results-rat}.

The \sseso{} latent at the final day of the window needs the same treatment even though it can
reach no fitted onset: incubation starts at lag~1, so its infections belong entirely to the
reset pipeline, and its conditional law is its Gamma prior.

\subsection{Latents at the conditioning day: the smoothing approximation}
\label{sec:smoothing}

For the latent-variable models, RAC at day $t$ in principle wants latents conditioned on the
history through $t$ --- a \emph{filtering} distribution --- whereas a single full-data fit
delivers the \emph{smoothed} distribution given the whole record.

\textbf{The full-data (smoothed) posterior is used for every $t$.} One fit per model then serves
every day of every curve, which keeps each analysis to a handful of MCMC runs rather than
several hundred. This is consistent with the analysis being fully retrospective: the parameters
are estimated from all the data by design, and the latents are treated the same way. The
consequence must be stated plainly --- the projection at day $t$ uses latents informed by data
after day $t$, so RAC$(t)$ is not literally a probability conditioned on the history through
$t$ for \ssi{}, \sseso{} and \ssiso{}. \dlo{} and \sse{} are unaffected: they have no latents.

The \emph{direction} of the effect is a hypothesis, not an assertion. Smoothing sharpens the
latents but does not push them uniformly one way: post-$t$ zeros are evidence that recent
cohorts had low infectivity and pull the retained state down, while post-$t$ positive
observations are evidence that earlier cohorts had high infectivity and pull it up. After the
final onset every subsequent observation is a zero, so the expectation is a predominantly
downward effect over the decision-relevant late period, and an ambiguous one earlier.

That hypothesis has been measured rather than assumed, using the particle filter's forward pass,
which produces filtering distributions as a by-product. Both signs appear, and where predicted.
For \ssi{} at a fixed parameter vector, smoothed minus filtering RAC is $+0.64$ around day~2 ---
where the filter has not yet seen the cases that reveal a high early infectivity --- and
negative after the last observed case, mean $-0.031$ and worst $-0.153$ around day~70
(\file{validation/results/rac_validation.md}). For the onset-anchored models the same
comparison, with the opposite sign convention, gives filtering minus smoothed RAC of about
$-0.63$ to $-0.66$ at its largest early and a mean of about $+0.013$ after the final onset
(\file{validation/results/onset_particle_mcmc_validation.md}). The late-period effect is
small enough not to move the threshold crossings by more than about a day, but it is a signed,
measured number rather than a claim.

\subsection{Reconstructing the latents the fit integrated out}
\label{sec:reconstruction}

The latent parameterisation of Section~\ref{sec:inference} integrates out, in closed form, every
latent that the data constrain only through the factor $\exp(-\sum_j\mu_j)$. Those latents are
not in the posterior draws, and on this series they are exactly the days from the last observed
onset onwards --- precisely the days a late conditioning day $t$ needs for its incubation
pipeline. Reading the retained state straight off the posterior array would look plausible and
be truncated at day~\resultnum{data.lastonsetday}, understating RAC for exactly the late days
where the decision is made.

The removed latents are therefore rebuilt from their exact conditional
$\Gam(k\cdot\text{scale}_u,\ k+c_u)$ before any risk is computed, and spliced onto the sampled
block to give a complete latent path by day. This is exact --- the removed latents are
conditionally independent of the sampled ones as well as of each other --- and it costs one
Gamma draw per removed latent per posterior draw, done once rather than once per $t$. The
reconstruction is validated end to end by a matched pair of fits: the default parameterisation,
which needs the reconstruction, against one that samples every latent, which does not. Their RAC
curves are indistinguishable, differing by at most $2.4\times10^{-3}$ --- the same spread as two
runs of the \emph{same} fit (\file{validation/results/rac_validation.md}).

\section{Inference}
\label{sec:inference}

\subsection{Sampling}

All fits are run in PyMC with NUTS. Each analysis uses
\resultnum{naivemodelsfixedk.chains} chains of \resultnum{naivemodelsfixedk.draws} draws after
\resultnum{naivemodelsfixedk.tune} tuning iterations, at a target acceptance probability of
\resultnum{naivemodelsfixedk.targetaccept} for the first three analyses and
\resultnum{onsetmodelsestimatedk.targetaccept} for the estimated-$k$ onset-anchored analysis.
Seeds live in the configuration file. One fit per (model, analysis) pair jointly estimates
$\Rpre$, $\Rpost$, any latents, and --- in Analyses 2 and 4 --- $k$.

\sse{} and \ssi{} appear in both the naive and the onset-anchored analyses. They are refitted
independently under each analysis's own seed, so their summaries can differ between figures by
Monte-Carlo error; no number is carried across.

\subsection{Priors}

Priors are proper and identical across the models within an analysis. This is not a stylistic
choice: the posterior model probabilities are Bayes factors, and diffuse or improper priors
would make them arbitrary.

\begin{itemize}
  \item $\Rpre$ and $\Rpost$: independent log-normals with median
    \resultnum{priors.R.median} and 95\% interval
    $(\resultnum{priors.R.quantilelower},\ \resultnum{priors.R.quantileupper})$.
  \item $k$, where estimated: a deliberately fairly informative log-normal with median
    \resultnum{priors.k.median} and 95\% interval
    $(\resultnum{priors.k.quantilelower},\ \resultnum{priors.k.quantileupper})$, the same in
    every model.
  \item Prior model probabilities: uniform over the models in each analysis, i.e.\
    $1/\resultnum{naivemodelsfixedk.nmodels}$ in Analyses 1--2 and
    $1/\resultnum{onsetmodelsfixedk.nmodels}$ in Analyses 3--4.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Identical priors are necessary but not sufficient for a fair comparison.}
Propriety makes the Bayes factors well defined; it does not make them \emph{neutral} when the
same symbol means different things in different models. $k$ is exactly such a symbol: an
individual-level offspring dispersion in \sse{} and \ssi{}, a day-level incidence dispersion in
\dlo{} (Section~\ref{sec:dlo-structure}). Placing the same prior on both is a deliberate choice,
and the model-probability panel of Fig.~\ref{fig:naive-fixed} in particular compares models one
of which is \emph{deliberately miscalibrated}, in order to demonstrate the error under study. It
is evidence about those specified models, not clean evidence about mechanism. Prior-predictive
calibration is out of scope here, so this caveat carries the weight instead.

\subsection{The latent block}

Sampling the latent Gammas directly is where NUTS struggles, badly: the \sseso{} shapes on this
series span several orders of magnitude down to $\sim10^{-6}$, and a Gamma coordinate that small
has a typical value outside the range of double precision, so it cannot be tuned into behaving.
Two mechanisms are used, and they compose.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Exact marginalisation.} A latent that reaches no observation day carrying a case
    enters the likelihood only through $\exp(-\sum_j\mu_j)$, which is linear in the latents and
    so factorises. Its Gamma prior is conjugate to that, giving
    $\int\Gam(y;k\,\text{scale},k)\,e^{-cy}\,\mathrm{d}y = (1+c/k)^{-k\,\text{scale}}$ and a
    conditional posterior $\Gam(k\,\text{scale},\ k+c)$. On this series that removes
    \resultnum{onsetmodelsfixedk.sseso.latents.marginalised} of \sseso{}'s
    \resultnum{onsetmodelsfixedk.sseso.latents.total} latents, leaving
    \resultnum{onsetmodelsfixedk.sseso.latents.sampled} sampled --- exactly the pathological
    tail, since the last case is on day~\resultnum{data.lastonsetday} --- and
    \resultnum{onsetmodelsfixedk.ssi.latents.marginalised} of \ssi{}'s and \ssiso{}'s
    \resultnum{onsetmodelsfixedk.ssi.latents.total}. It is an exact operation, and
    Section~\ref{sec:reconstruction} describes how the removed latents are recovered where the
    risk calculators need them.
  \item \textbf{Inverse-CDF reparameterisation.} The surviving latents are sampled as
    $\mathrm{Uniform}(0,1)$ variates pushed through the Gamma quantile function, which gives a
    bounded, flat-prior, constant-geometry parameter space and does have a gradient in the
    PyTensor in use, so it runs under NUTS.
\end{itemize}

The choice rests on a benchmark of 74 MCMC runs
(\file{validation/results/sampler_benchmark.md}). Its main negative finding is worth stating
because it constrains what else could be tried: this is a \emph{geometry} problem, not a warm-up
problem. Mean-1 rescaling of the latents leaves the real \sseso{} model with 3999 of 4000 draws
divergent, exactly as the untouched model does, and more tuning makes $\hat{R}$ worse rather
than better --- rescaling is a translation on the log scale, so it leaves the curvature
untouched. The inverse-CDF reparameterisation takes the same model from 4000 divergences to
none.

\paragraph{Convergence.} Across all models of all four analyses, the worst
$\hat{R}$ for the reported scalars is \resultnum{overall.diagnostics.maxrhat}, the
smallest bulk effective sample size is
\resultnum{overall.diagnostics.minbulkess}, and the total number of divergent
transitions is \resultnum{overall.diagnostics.divergences}. Per analysis, the
minimum bulk effective sample sizes are
\resultnum{naivemodelsfixedk.diagnostics.minbulkess} (Analysis 1),
\resultnum{naivemodelsestimatedk.diagnostics.minbulkess} (Analysis 2),
\resultnum{onsetmodelsfixedk.diagnostics.minbulkess} (Analysis 3) and
\resultnum{onsetmodelsestimatedk.diagnostics.minbulkess} (Analysis 4), with no divergences in
any of them.

\subsection{Model evidence and posterior model probabilities}

The marginal likelihood $p(D_{1:\resultnum{data.withdrawalday}}\mid\text{model})$ is computed
with the parameters \emph{and} the latents integrated out, from the built model's own joint
log-density rather than from a re-derivation of the likelihood, so that the integral estimated
is the one that was sampled. The integral is done in PyMC's unconstrained coordinates with the
Jacobian included; getting the transform's direction wrong would shift every evidence by a
constant --- a perfectly plausible number and a wrong Bayes factor --- so that is pinned by test
rather than trusted. Bridge sampling is the estimator; prior Monte Carlo and importance sampling
are kept as agreement checks, and deterministic quadrature as the exact answer where the space
is small enough.

Three checks establish that the numbers are right rather than merely self-consistent
(\file{validation/results/evidence_validation.md}): bridge sampling reproduces deterministic
quadrature for \dlo{} and \sse{} on the real series to $1.1\times10^{-3}$ and
$2.9\times10^{-3}$ nats, with the quadrature box's boundary density 41--44 nats below its peak;
the three estimators agree on \ssi{}, all within their own error bars; and the evidence is
invariant to the latent parameterisation, which is what shows the marginalisation carries the
whole removed factor rather than just its shape.

\subsection{Comparing the dispersion posteriors}

Where $k$ is estimated, the models are handed the \emph{same} prior on it, so wherever their
posteriors end up apart is a measurement rather than a demonstration. Three complementary
measures are reported for every pair, because none says enough alone: the \textbf{median ratio}
(location, but not width); the \textbf{overlap} $\int\min(p_a,p_b)$ (scale-free, symmetric, and
finite where a Kullback--Leibler divergence would not be); and $P(k_a>k_b)$ for independent
draws. All are computed on the log scale, as the density panels are drawn; the overlap is
invariant to that choice. Every pair is compared rather than a designated reference model.

\subsection{Independent validation of the pipeline}

Two particle-filter checks answer different questions and are kept separate. At fixed
$(\Rpre,\Rpost,k)$, with conditioning matched to the pipeline, the filter is an equality check
on the RAC arithmetic; agreement is required and is obtained, to $3.2\times10^{-3}$ for \ssi{}
and $0.021$ for the onset-anchored models. Over the parameters, particle marginal
Metropolis--Hastings gives an independent route to the posterior that shares no machinery with
the PyMC fits --- the main guard against the latent-parameterisation risk, which
simulation-based calibration could not catch, since it validates an implementation against
itself. PMMH and PyMC 95\% intervals overlap for all six synthetic parameter comparisons, and
the largest measured variance of the estimated log-likelihood is $0.081$ at 250 particles,
comfortably inside the range PMMH tolerates. A PMMH RAC curve should \emph{not} match the MCMC
one for the latent models: the filter conditions on filtering latents where the pipeline uses
smoothed ones, and that gap is the Section~\ref{sec:smoothing} measurement rather than a defect.

Externally, the Poisson-limit RAC reproduces the published \'Equateur risk curve of Thompson et
al.\ (2024) exactly under their conventions, which differ from this project's by exactly one
day: their published $R$ posterior for this outbreak is recovered, and the two risk curves agree
to $2.2\times10^{-4}$ over 200\,000 draws (\file{validation/results/rac_validation.md}).
Particle MCMC remains a check throughout and never a results path; the main analyses stay in
PyMC.

    not at all.

    correct answers to different questions, and the end-of-outbreak criterion in use here asks

    independent evidence about mechanism. Analysis 2 separates the two readings.
  \item \textbf{$\Rpost$ is comparable across the three models} ---
    \resultnum{naivemodelsfixedk.dlo.rpost.interval},
    \resultnum{naivemodelsfixedk.sse.rpost.interval} and
    \resultnum{naivemodelsfixedk.ssi.rpost.interval} for \dlo{}, \sse{} and \ssi{} (median and

    the aggregate infectivity of $n$ i.i.d.\ individuals is $\Gam(kn,k)$ whichever $n$ they are:
    regrouping moves the cohorts, not the variance. This is a mechanism proposed for the
    measurement, not a second measurement --- Analysis 2 has no non-naive arm, so it cannot
    separate this from the purely structural difference of Section~\ref{sec:dlo-structure}.
    Analysis 4 is where it is tested.

    \resultnum{naivemodelsestimatedk.dlo.evidencegain} to \dlo{}, but only
    \resultnum{naivemodelsestimatedk.ssi.evidencegain} to \ssi{}. Analysis 1 was charging \dlo{}
    and \sse{} for a value that was never theirs, and \resultnum{naivemodelsfixedk.fixedk} really
    is \ssi{}'s number on this series.
  \item \textbf{The risk gap survives, which is the point of the Fig.~\ref{fig:naive-fixed} /
    Fig.~\ref{fig:naive-estimated} pair.} Each model now sits at its own best $k$, and \dlo{}
    still crosses 0.05 on day~\resultnum{naivemodelsestimatedk.dlo.rac05.day} against
    day~\resultnum{naivemodelsestimatedk.sse.rac05.day} for \sse{} and
    day~\resultnum{naivemodelsestimatedk.ssi.rac05.day} for \ssi{}, and still
    \textbf{\resultnum{naivemodelsestimatedk.dlo.rac01.day}} reaches 0.01 inside the window,
    where \sse{} does on day~\resultnum{naivemodelsestimatedk.sse.rac01.day} and \ssi{} on
    day~\resultnum{naivemodelsestimatedk.ssi.rac01.day}. Misapplying a literature $k$ makes the
    gap; the day-level mechanism keeps it.
  \item \textbf{The posterior model probabilities are unchanged in direction} --- \ssi{}
    \resultnum{naivemodelsestimatedk.ssi.probability}, \dlo{}
    \resultnum{naivemodelsestimatedk.dlo.probability}, \sse{}
    \resultnum{naivemodelsestimatedk.sse.probability} --- and now carry a cleaner reading, since
    each model has been allowed its own dispersion.
\end{itemize}

\section{Analyses 3 and 4 --- onset anchoring}
\label{sec:results-onset}

\begin{figure}[htbp]

    Fig.~\ref{fig:naive-fixed}, with a uniform prior over
    \resultnum{onsetmodelsfixedk.nmodels} models in \textbf{C}. All four models are driven by the
    same onset-to-onset serial interval, so the comparison isolates anchoring.}
  \label{fig:onset-fixed}
\end{figure}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{../figures/onset_models_estimated_k/onset_models_estimated_k.pdf}
  \caption{\textbf{Onset anchoring with $k$ estimated --- the test of the Analysis 2
    prediction.} Panels as in Fig.~\ref{fig:naive-estimated}. \textbf{C} is where the
    attenuation hypothesis is settled: \sseso{}'s $k$ posterior should sit below naive \sse{}'s
    if the incubation convolution is what inflates day-level dispersion estimates, while
    \ssiso{}'s should barely move from \ssi{}'s.}
  \label{fig:onset-estimated}
\end{figure}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Onset anchoring advances both RAC crossings by 8--12 days, and this is the
    headline.} With $k$ estimated (Fig.~\ref{fig:onset-estimated}), \sseso{} crosses 0.05 on
    day~\resultnum{onsetmodelsestimatedk.sseso.rac05.day} against \sse{}'s
    day~\resultnum{onsetmodelsestimatedk.sse.rac05.day} --- an advance of
    \resultnum{onsetmodelsestimatedk.sseso-vs-sse.shift05} days --- and 0.01 on
    day~\resultnum{onsetmodelsestimatedk.sseso.rac01.day} against
    day~\resultnum{onsetmodelsestimatedk.sse.rac01.day}, an advance of
    \resultnum{onsetmodelsestimatedk.sseso-vs-sse.shift01} days. \ssiso{} advances \ssi{}'s
    crossings by \resultnum{onsetmodelsestimatedk.ssiso-vs-ssi.shift05} and
    \resultnum{onsetmodelsestimatedk.ssiso-vs-ssi.shift01} days respectively
    (day~\resultnum{onsetmodelsestimatedk.ssiso.rac05.day} against
    day~\resultnum{onsetmodelsestimatedk.ssi.rac05.day}, and
    day~\resultnum{onsetmodelsestimatedk.ssiso.rac01.day} against
    day~\resultnum{onsetmodelsestimatedk.ssi.rac01.day}). At a fixed $k$
    (Fig.~\ref{fig:onset-fixed}) the same comparisons give
    \resultnum{onsetmodelsfixedk.sseso-vs-sse.shift05} and
    \resultnum{onsetmodelsfixedk.sseso-vs-sse.shift01} days for \sseso{} against \sse{}, and
    \resultnum{onsetmodelsfixedk.ssiso-vs-ssi.shift05} and
    \resultnum{onsetmodelsfixedk.ssiso-vs-ssi.shift01} days for \ssiso{} against \ssi{}. The
    displacement is on the scale of the mean incubation period,
    \resultnum{delays.incubation.mean}\,d.
  \item \textbf{Why the direction is \emph{earlier}, and why it is not a shorter generation
    time.} All four models share the same onset-to-onset serial interval; what changes is which
    side of the conditioning day a transmission falls on once that interval is cut into
    transmission and incubation pieces. The onset-anchored models place infection at transmission
    time, so infections already generated under the historical $\Rpost$ stay in the incubation
    pipeline when controls are reset, and the remaining TOST opportunity of older onset cohorts is
    already largely spent. The naive models move that infection decision to the later
    symptom-onset date and apply the reset $\Rpre$ there, which makes the residual state look
    younger and drain later.
  \item \textbf{The same indexing error inflates naive $\Rpost$ threefold.} With $k$ estimated,
    the posterior medians (95\% CrI) are \resultnum{onsetmodelsestimatedk.sse.rpost.interval}
    for \sse{} and \resultnum{onsetmodelsestimatedk.ssi.rpost.interval} for \ssi{}, against
    \resultnum{onsetmodelsestimatedk.sseso.rpost.interval} for \sseso{} and
    \resultnum{onsetmodelsestimatedk.ssiso.rpost.interval} for \ssiso{}. The naive convention
    applies the day-\resultnum{data.ertarrivalday} switch to \emph{onsets}, so post-arrival
    onsets that arose from pre-arrival infections are charged to the controlled period. $\Rpre$
    is much more stable across the four --- \resultnum{onsetmodelsestimatedk.sse.rpre.interval},
    \resultnum{onsetmodelsestimatedk.ssi.rpre.interval},
    \resultnum{onsetmodelsestimatedk.sseso.rpre.interval} and
    \resultnum{onsetmodelsestimatedk.ssiso.rpre.interval} --- which is what one expects if the
    effect is about \emph{when} the switch lands rather than about the epidemic's early growth.
  \item \textbf{The Analysis 2 attenuation prediction passes.} \sseso{}'s $k$ posterior,
    \resultnum{onsetmodelsestimatedk.sseso.k.interval}, sits below naive \sse{}'s
    \resultnum{onsetmodelsestimatedk.sse.k.interval} and towards the literature value: median
    ratio \resultnum{onsetmodelsestimatedk.sse-vs-sseso.medianratio}, overlap
    \resultnum{onsetmodelsestimatedk.sse-vs-sseso.overlap},
    $P(k_{\msse}>k_{\msseso})=
     \resultnum{onsetmodelsestimatedk.sse-vs-sseso.probabilitygreater}$. \ssi{} moves much less
    --- \resultnum{onsetmodelsestimatedk.ssi.k.interval} against \ssiso{}'s
    \resultnum{onsetmodelsestimatedk.ssiso.k.interval}, overlap
    \resultnum{onsetmodelsestimatedk.ssi-vs-ssiso.overlap}. Incubation convolution attenuates
    \emph{day-level} dispersion when onsets are treated as infections; individual-level
    dispersion is comparatively stable to regrouping, exactly as predicted.
  \item \textbf{Once anchoring is made explicit, the mechanism stops mattering for $k$.}
    \sseso{} and \ssiso{} agree almost exactly --- overlap
    \resultnum{onsetmodelsestimatedk.sseso-vs-ssiso.overlap}, median ratio
    \resultnum{onsetmodelsestimatedk.sseso-vs-ssiso.medianratio},
    $P(k_{\msseso}>k_{\mssiso})=
     \resultnum{onsetmodelsestimatedk.sseso-vs-ssiso.probabilitygreater}$ --- where their naive
    counterparts differ by a factor of
    \resultnum{onsetmodelsestimatedk.sse-vs-ssi.medianratio} at an overlap of
    \resultnum{onsetmodelsestimatedk.sse-vs-ssi.overlap}. The event-versus-individual gap in
    Analysis 2 was to a large extent the conflation, not the mechanism.
  \item \textbf{The onset-anchored models are strongly preferred, and the two of them are
    indistinguishable.} With $k$ estimated, the posterior model probabilities are
    \resultnum{onsetmodelsestimatedk.sseso.probability} for \sseso{} and
    \resultnum{onsetmodelsestimatedk.ssiso.probability} for \ssiso{}, against
    \resultnum{onsetmodelsestimatedk.ssi.probability} for \ssi{} and
    \resultnum{onsetmodelsestimatedk.sse.probability} for \sse{}; the log evidences are
    \resultnum{onsetmodelsestimatedk.sseso.logevidence},
    \resultnum{onsetmodelsestimatedk.ssiso.logevidence},
    \resultnum{onsetmodelsestimatedk.ssi.logevidence} and
    \resultnum{onsetmodelsestimatedk.sse.logevidence} nats. The fixed-$k$ analysis gives the same
    ordering (\resultnum{onsetmodelsfixedk.sseso.probability},
    \resultnum{onsetmodelsfixedk.ssiso.probability},
    \resultnum{onsetmodelsfixedk.ssi.probability},
    \resultnum{onsetmodelsfixedk.sse.probability}).
  \item \textbf{The onset-anchored models gain nothing from estimating $k$, which is a
    consistency check on the previous two bullets.} Releasing $k$ is worth
    \resultnum{onsetmodelsestimatedk.sseso.evidencegain} nats to \sseso{} and
    \resultnum{onsetmodelsestimatedk.ssiso.evidencegain} to \ssiso{} --- within their own
    estimation error --- against \resultnum{onsetmodelsestimatedk.sse.evidencegain} for naive
    \sse{}. Their $k$ posteriors already sit on \resultnum{onsetmodelsfixedk.fixedk}, so fixing
    it there costs them nothing.
  \item \textbf{Do not universalise the direction of the 8--12-day advance.} It is measured
    under this reset convention, this switch convention, this outbreak history and this
    posterior. The incubation-scale explanation is the mechanism for \emph{this} result, not a
    theorem for arbitrary time series or interventions.
\end{itemize}

\section{Supplementary --- RAC against RAT, and the incubation pipeline}
\label{sec:results-rat}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{../figures/onset_models_rat/onset_models_rat.pdf}
  \caption{\textbf{The gap between the risk of additional cases and the risk of additional
    transmission is the incubation pipeline, quantified.} \sseso{} and \ssiso{} with $k$ fixed
    at \resultnum{onsetmodelsfixedk.fixedk} (\textbf{A}) and estimated (\textbf{B}). Dashed:
    RAC. Solid: RAT. The shaded region between them is the contribution of individuals already
    infected on the conditioning day but not yet symptomatic. Under the three naive models these
    two curves coincide by assumption, which is the conflation being measured.}
  \label{fig:rat}
\end{figure}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{The pipeline is worth about a quarter of a unit of probability at its widest.}
    With $k$ estimated, RAC exceeds RAT by at most
    \resultnum{onsetmodelsestimatedk.sseso.gap.max} for \sseso{}, on
    day~\resultnum{onsetmodelsestimatedk.sseso.gap.day}
    (\resultnum{onsetmodelsestimatedk.sseso.gap.date}), and by at most
    \resultnum{onsetmodelsestimatedk.ssiso.gap.max} for \ssiso{}, on
    day~\resultnum{onsetmodelsestimatedk.ssiso.gap.day}. The gap peaks shortly after the last
    observed onset (day~\resultnum{data.lastonsetday}), which is when the pipeline is at its
    largest relative to the transmission still to come, and closes as it drains.
  \item \textbf{It delays a case-based declaration by about a working week.} With $k$ estimated,
    \sseso{}'s RAT settles below 0.05 on
    day~\resultnum{onsetmodelsestimatedk.sseso.rat05.day} and below 0.01 on
    day~\resultnum{onsetmodelsestimatedk.sseso.rat01.day}, against
    day~\resultnum{onsetmodelsestimatedk.sseso.rac05.day} and
    day~\resultnum{onsetmodelsestimatedk.sseso.rac01.day} for RAC --- later by
    \resultnum{onsetmodelsestimatedk.sseso.gap.shift05} and
    \resultnum{onsetmodelsestimatedk.sseso.gap.shift01} days. For \ssiso{} the same comparison
    gives \resultnum{onsetmodelsestimatedk.ssiso.gap.shift05} and
    \resultnum{onsetmodelsestimatedk.ssiso.gap.shift01} days
    (RAT on days \resultnum{onsetmodelsestimatedk.ssiso.rat05.day} and
    \resultnum{onsetmodelsestimatedk.ssiso.rat01.day} against RAC on days
    \resultnum{onsetmodelsestimatedk.ssiso.rac05.day} and
    \resultnum{onsetmodelsestimatedk.ssiso.rac01.day}).
  \item \textbf{The two quantities answer different questions and the difference is
    decision-relevant.} RAT isolates what relaxing interventions does; RAC is what a surveillance
    system would actually see. A criterion written on cases keeps interventions in place while
    the pipeline drains, even though transmission risk has already settled --- and a naive model,
    which identifies the two by assumption, cannot represent that distinction at all.
  \item \textbf{Fixing $k$ changes very little here}, as the near-identical panels show: the
    widest gap is \resultnum{onsetmodelsfixedk.sseso.gap.max} for \sseso{} and
    \resultnum{onsetmodelsfixedk.ssiso.gap.max} for \ssiso{}, against
    \resultnum{onsetmodelsestimatedk.sseso.gap.max} and
    \resultnum{onsetmodelsestimatedk.ssiso.gap.max} when it is estimated. This follows from the
    onset-anchored $k$ posteriors already sitting on the literature value.
\end{itemize}

\section{Sensitivity analyses}
\label{sec:sensitivity}

None of the following has been run. They are listed in rough order of how much each would add,
with what it would resolve.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Outbreak-specific serial interval} (mean
    \resultnum{delays.alternativeserialinterval.mean}\,d, SD
    \resultnum{delays.alternativeserialinterval.sd}\,d) --- \emph{naive models only}. Its much
    lighter tail materially lowers the RAC after a long case-free period, and it is the estimate
    that should be preferred where outbreak-specific contact-tracing data exist. It is
    structurally inadmissible for the onset-anchored models (Section~\ref{sec:data}), and that
    incompatibility should be reported rather than worked around: it says something about how
    much structure the incubation/TOST decomposition imposes.
  \item \textbf{Incubation/TOST decomposition.} Hold $w$ fixed and re-run \sseso{} and \ssiso{}
    under two or three other published EVD incubation estimates, letting the budget check supply
    the matching TOST each time. This bounds how much of the onset-anchoring result rests on the
    particular split used here, which the serial interval alone does not identify. One
    configuration value; no new code.
  \item \textbf{Filtering rather than smoothed latents.} Section~\ref{sec:smoothing} reports the
    signed comparison at a fixed parameter vector; repeating it integrated over the parameter
    posterior would turn the stated approximation into a quantified one across all three latent
    models.
  \item \textbf{Shifted $R$-switch for the naive models.} The onset-anchoring comparison
    confounds model structure with the indexing convention for the intervention. Re-running the
    naive models with the switch moved back by $\mathbb{E}[A]=\resultnum{delays.incubation.mean}$
    days separates them: any residual difference from the onset-anchored models is then
    attributable to structure rather than indexing. One configuration value.
  \item \textbf{Non-empty initial incubation pipeline} for the onset-anchored models. Seed one or
    two already-infected individuals at day~\resultnum{data.firstday} with residual incubation
    drawn from $\finc$, and check the effect on $\Rpre$ and on the RAC curve. This is the
    sensitivity that matters if the index case is not epidemiologically identified
    (Section~\ref{sec:data}).
  \item \textbf{Prior sensitivity}, in particular for $k$, whose prior is deliberately
    informative and whose role differs between models.
\end{enumerate}
