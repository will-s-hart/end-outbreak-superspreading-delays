\begin{abstract}
\noindent
Deciding when an outbreak is over requires estimating the probability that further cases will
occur if control measures are relaxed. This estimate depends on two often-implicit choices: how
transmission heterogeneity is represented and whether symptom-onset dates are treated as if they
were infection dates. We compared five renewal models of the 2018 \'Equateur Province Ebola
outbreak, all calibrated to the same onset-to-onset serial interval, and calculated the risk of
additional cases (RAC) after each day. Models placing overdispersion at the level of the day,
transmission event, or individual produced materially different risks at the same nominal
dispersion parameter and inferred different values when that parameter was estimated. Models
that anchored transmission to the infector's symptom onset and represented incubation explicitly
crossed both decision thresholds a week or more earlier than their infection-anchored
counterparts at a common dispersion parameter, and between four and fifteen days earlier when
that parameter was estimated; they also inferred substantially smaller post-intervention
reproduction numbers. Finally,
distinguishing additional cases from additional transmission isolated the contribution of people
already infected but not yet symptomatic---a component that infection-anchored models cannot
represent. End-of-outbreak assessments therefore depend not only on the amount of transmission
heterogeneity, but also on its mechanism and on the time scale to which transmission is anchored.
\end{abstract}

% =============================================================================================
\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}
% =============================================================================================

An end-of-outbreak declaration is a decision under uncertainty: interventions are withdrawn
when the probability of further cases is judged acceptably small. Renewal models provide a
natural basis for that decision by linking observed incidence to past transmission and projecting
forward under relaxed control. Two choices within these models are seldom examined together.

\paragraph{The mechanism of overdispersion.} Transmission of Ebola virus disease, like that of
many pathogens, is highly heterogeneous, and this is usually represented by a single dispersion
parameter $k$. Models can share a mean--variance relationship at the population level while
differing in \emph{where} the excess variance sits --- in a day's aggregate incidence, in each
transmission event, or in each infected individual. Those are different statements about the
world, and we show they imply different end-of-outbreak risks. They also make $k$ a different
quantity in each case, so a literature estimate obtained under one mechanism is not portable to
another. In particular, an individual-level estimate such as
$k = \resultnum{naivemodelsfixedk.fixedk}$ does not retain its interpretation in a day-level
incidence model.

\paragraph{Onset dates are not infection dates.} Renewal models are routinely fitted with a
symptom-onset time series substituted directly for an infection time series. This conflates two
distinct events separated by an incubation period, which for Ebola virus disease has a mean of
about \resultnum{delays.incubation.mean} days. We quantify the consequences by comparing
infection-anchored models with explicitly onset-anchored counterparts, in which transmission is
referenced to the infector's own onset and the incubation period is modelled.

We compare five models using the 2018 \'Equateur Province Ebola outbreak. All use the same
onset-to-onset serial interval, allowing differences to be attributed to transmission mechanism
and temporal anchoring rather than to different interval assumptions. We first compare three
infection-anchored mechanisms with the dispersion fixed and estimated, then compare event- and
individual-level models with onset-anchored counterparts. The primary estimand is the risk of
additional cases; we then ask whether the anchoring comparison holds for the risks of additional
transmission and of sustained transmission too, and under incomplete reporting. Sensitivity analyses needed to assess the robustness of these comparisons are
specified separately.

\section{Data and delay distributions}
\label{sec:data}

\subsection{The 2018 \'Equateur outbreak}
\label{sec:outbreak}

The observed series is a daily count of Ebola virus disease cases by \emph{symptom-onset date}
for the 2018 \'Equateur Province outbreak in the Democratic Republic of the Congo:
\resultnum{data.totalcases} cases, with onsets on \resultnum{data.onsetdays} distinct days
between \resultnum{data.firstdate} and \resultnum{data.lastonsetdate}, and at most
\resultnum{data.maxdailyonsets} onsets on any one day. An Ebola Response Team (ERT) was present
from \resultnum{data.ertarrivaldate} and withdrew on \resultnum{data.withdrawaldate}. Indexing
days from the first observed onset:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lcl}
  \toprule
  & Day index & Date \\
  \midrule
  First onset (index case)      & \resultnum{data.firstday}         & \resultnum{data.firstdate} \\
  ERT arrival                   & \resultnum{data.ertarrivalday}    & \resultnum{data.ertarrivaldate} \\
  Last observed onset           & \resultnum{data.lastonsetday}     & \resultnum{data.lastonsetdate} \\
  ERT withdrawal                & \resultnum{data.withdrawalday}    & \resultnum{data.withdrawaldate} \\
  End of the analysis window    & \resultnum{data.lastday}          & \resultnum{data.lastdate} \\
  \bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent
The analysis window is days $0$--$\resultnum{data.lastday}$, i.e.\
\resultnum{data.days} days, with \resultnum{data.preertcases} cases before the ERT arrived and
\resultnum{data.postertcases} after. The series is padded with zero counts from the last
observed onset to the end of the window. These case-free days provide the evidence that drives
the estimated risk down. The main analyses take the series at face value;
Section~\ref{sec:results-underreporting} relaxes that and treats the observed onsets as a
reported fraction of the true ones.

The window runs \resultnum{data.extradays} days past the ERT's withdrawal, and no case occurred
in \'Equateur over those days. They are there so that every risk curve reported below settles
below both decision thresholds inside the window: ending at the withdrawal left several curves
still above $0.01$ on the final day, which bounds a crossing from below without locating it.
\textbf{One caveat follows, and it is a limitation rather than a modelling choice.} $\Rpost$
applies to every day from the ERT's arrival onwards, so the likelihood charges those last
\resultnum{data.extradays} case-free days to the controlled regime although the response had in
fact ended. The parameter posteriors, the model evidences and the dispersion summaries below are
therefore conditioned on a record whose final days are counterfactual in that one respect. The
effect is small enough to be invisible at the precision quoted --- refitting the fixed-$k$
analysis to the withdrawal day instead moves every $\Rpost$ median by less than $0.005$ and
every log evidence by less than the bridge-sampling standard error --- and the risk curve for a
conditioning day beyond the withdrawal answers, by construction, what the risk would have been
had the response been held in place until that day.

Day~$0$ is treated as an initial condition in every model, so likelihoods run over days
$1,\dots,\resultnum{data.lastday}$. Conditioning on a single imported index case asserts
that nothing was transmitting or incubating beforehand. For the infection-anchored models this
is mild --- the renewal recursion simply starts from one case. For the onset-anchored models it
is stronger, because it also asserts an \emph{empty incubation pipeline} at day~$0$: anyone
infected shortly before the index case's onset would contribute onsets in the first weeks that
the model can explain only as transmission from the index case, inflating $\Rpre$. The
onset-anchored models are therefore more sensitive to whether the first observed case is the true
epidemiological index case.

\subsection{Serial interval}
\label{sec:serial-interval}

We use a generic Ebola serial-interval estimate, represented by a discretised gamma distribution
with mean \resultnum{delays.serialinterval.mean}\,d and standard deviation
\resultnum{delays.serialinterval.sd}\,d, in all five models. An outbreak-specific estimate fitted
to contact-traced pairs from this outbreak has mean
\resultnum{delays.alternativeserialinterval.mean}\,d and standard deviation
\resultnum{delays.alternativeserialinterval.sd}\,d.

We selected the generic estimate because it is compatible with the variance budget of
Section~\ref{sec:decomposition}. Splitting
an onset-to-onset interval into an incubation period and a time-from-onset-to-transmission
(TOST) delay requires the two variances to sum to the serial interval's. The generic estimate
has variance \resultnum{delays.serialinterval.variance}\,d$^2$, which comfortably accommodates
standard published Ebola incubation estimates. The outbreak-specific estimate has variance
\resultnum{delays.alternativeserialinterval.variance}\,d$^2$, \emph{smaller} than the incubation
variance alone (\resultnum{delays.incubation.variance}\,d$^2$), and is therefore structurally
inadmissible for the onset-anchored models under the assumed independent delay decomposition.
Sensitivity to the outbreak-specific estimate can therefore be assessed only for the
infection-anchored models.

\subsection{Discretisation, indexing and the decomposition}
\label{sec:decomposition}

Continuous delays are gamma distributed and discretised by the method of Cori et al.\ (2013):
the mass at integer lag $s$ is $\int_{s-1}^{s+1}(1-|s-u|)\,g(u)\,\mathrm{d}u$, normalised. Three
discrete delays are used, and they differ in the lag at which they start:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{llll}
  \toprule
  Distribution & Weights & First lag & Interpretation \\
  \midrule
  Serial interval        & $w_s$        & $1$ & onset of infector $\to$ onset of infectee \\
  Incubation period      & $\finc_a$    & $1$ & infection $\to$ onset \\
  Onset to transmission  & $\ftost_s$   & $0$ & onset of infector $\to$ transmission \\
  \bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent
Write $F_r = \sum_{s=1}^{r} w_s$ with $F_0 = 0$ for the serial-interval distribution function,
and let
\begin{equation}
  \stost_r = \Pr(S > r) = 1 - \sum_{s=0}^{r}\ftost_s,
  \qquad
  \sinc_r = \Pr(A > r) = 1 - \sum_{a=1}^{r}\finc_a
  \label{eq:survivals}
\end{equation}
be the survival functions of the TOST delay $S$ and the incubation period $A$. Note
$\sinc_0 = 1$ but $\stost_0 = 1 - \ftost_0 \le 1$: a case has all of its incubation ahead of it
on the day it is infected, but has already had its lag-$0$ transmission opportunity on the day
it becomes symptomatic.

The lag-$0$ rule is what makes the onset-anchored recursion causal. Because $\finc$ places no
mass at lag~$0$, an infection on day $t$ cannot produce an onset before day $t+1$, so a day's
onset cohort is fully determined before that day's own transmission is evaluated. The TOST
distribution \emph{may} place mass at lag $0$, so a case can transmit on its own onset day. At
most one of the two may be supported at lag~$0$: if both were, infection, onset and onward
transmission could all fall within one day and the recursion would not be well ordered.

\paragraph{The variance budget.} For an infector with onset on day $u$, transmission occurs on
day $u+S$ and the infectee's onset on day $u+S+A$, so the onset-to-onset interval is $S+A$ and
matching the target serial interval requires
\begin{equation}
  \mathbb{E}[S]+\mathbb{E}[A] = \resultnum{delays.serialinterval.mean},
  \qquad
  \mathrm{Var}[S]+\mathrm{Var}[A] = \resultnum{delays.serialinterval.variance}.
\end{equation}
The incubation period is fixed from a published Ebola estimate --- mean
\resultnum{delays.incubation.mean}\,d, standard deviation \resultnum{delays.incubation.sd}\,d
(WHO Ebola Response Team, 2014) --- and $\ftost$ is the gamma carrying the residual mean and
variance, here mean \resultnum{delays.tost.mean}\,d and standard deviation
\resultnum{delays.tost.sd}\,d. A negative residual variance would make the incubation estimate
inadmissible against the serial interval. Discretising and then convolving is not quite the same
operation as convolving and then discretising, so we verified the decomposition numerically: the
discrete convolution $\ftost * \finc$ differs from the discretised target $w$ by
\resultnum{delays.discrepancy} in total variation. All delays are truncated at lag
\resultnum{delays.maxlag}, the length of the analysis window, so nothing is truncated within it
(Fig.~\ref{fig:delays}).

\begin{remark}
The convolution is faithful, but \emph{how} the mean and variance are split between incubation
and TOST is assumed rather than identified by the serial interval alone. The sensitivity of the
onset-anchoring results to this decomposition must therefore be assessed explicitly.
\end{remark}

\section{Models}
\label{sec:models}
% =============================================================================================

Figure~\ref{fig:schematic} sets out the two anchoring conventions and the three things that
follow from the choice between them: how a transmission pair is dated, what state survives the
last day of the record, and when the reproduction number switches. The rest of this section
states the models formally.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{../figures/model_schematic/model_schematic.pdf}
  \caption{\textbf{What separates the infection- and onset-anchored models.} A schematic:
    positions are illustrative and no panel is drawn from data. \textbf{A}: one
    infector--infectee pair. The infection-anchored models connect the two recorded dates
    directly through the serial interval, reading each onset date as an infection date; the
    onset-anchored models route the same interval through an unobserved infection, splitting it
    into an onset-to-transmission delay and an incubation period, $w = \ftost * \finc$
    (Section~\ref{sec:decomposition}). \textbf{B}, \textbf{C}: the state carried past the last
    day of the record. Both conventions retain the transmission that observed cases have still to
    come, $\Lambda(t)$ or $W(t)$; only the onset-anchored one also carries a pipeline of
    individuals already infected but not yet symptomatic, of mean $M(t)$, which makes a further
    case possible without further transmission and so separates $\RAC$ from $\RAT$
    (Section~\ref{sec:onset-risk}). Setting $M(t) = 0$ recovers the infection-anchored
    expressions. \textbf{D}: the day $R$ switches, in each convention's own time index. The
    onset-anchored models change $R$ for the transmission occurring on the intervention date, so
    the recorded onsets respond an incubation period later; the infection-anchored models change
    it for the onsets recorded on that date, which attributes the change to transmission that took
    place a mean incubation period --- \resultnum{delays.incubation.mean}\,d --- earlier
    (Section~\ref{sec:switch}). The Poisson forms \cori{} and \coriso{} are drawn: overdispersion
    changes the exponents in \textbf{B} and \textbf{C}, not the structure.}
  \label{fig:schematic}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Notation}
\label{sec:notation}

Let $C_t$ be the observed count on day $t$. The three \emph{infection-anchored} models set
$I_t := C_t$, reading onset dates as infection dates --- the practice under study.
The two \emph{onset-anchored} models set $D_t := C_t$ and treat onsets as onsets, with
infections and onsets separated by the incubation period. The reproduction number $R_t$ takes
the value $\Rpre$ before the ERT arrives and $\Rpost$ from its arrival onwards
(Section~\ref{sec:switch}), and $k > 0$ is a dispersion parameter, small $k$ corresponding to
high heterogeneity.

\begin{remark}
$\NB(\text{mean}=\mu,\ \text{disp}=\kappa)$ denotes the negative binomial with mean $\mu$,
variance $\mu + \mu^2/\kappa$ and probability generating function
$G(s) = \bigl(\kappa/(\kappa+\mu(1-s))\bigr)^{\kappa}$; in particular
$G(0) = (1+\mu/\kappa)^{-\kappa}$. $\Gam(\alpha,\beta)$ has shape $\alpha$, rate $\beta$ and
mean $\alpha/\beta$.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Infection-anchored models}
\label{sec:naive-models}

The Poisson baseline and the three compared infection-anchored models share one generative
skeleton. Cases beget cases through the serial interval: an individual infected on day $u$
produces secondary infections that appear on day $u+\ell$ with probability $w_\ell$, and the
reproduction number in force on the day a secondary case appears sets their expected number.
Write
\begin{equation}
  \lambda_t = \sum_{s\ge 1} w_s I_{t-s}
  \label{eq:naive-foi}
\end{equation}
for the force of infection driving day $t$. What distinguishes the models is the offspring law
--- where, if anywhere, the excess variance sits.

\paragraph{From transmission to observations.} Infection and symptom onset are identified, so
$I_t=C_t$. The transmission law for $I_t$ is therefore also the likelihood for the observed
daily counts; no incubation step intervenes.

\paragraph{Poisson baseline: \cori{}.} An individual infected on day $u$ generates, at
each lag $\ell$ and independently of every other, a Poisson number of offspring with mean
$R_{u+\ell}w_\ell$. Poisson superposition then gives the day-$t$ total as
\begin{equation}
  \text{\cori{}}: \qquad I_t \mid \lambda_t \sim \Pois\!\left(R_t\lambda_t\right).
  \label{eq:cori}
\end{equation}
This is the standard renewal model of Cori et al.\ (2013) and the model underlying most published
end-of-outbreak risk calculations. It has no dispersion parameter.

\paragraph{Transmission-event heterogeneity: \sse{}.} The offspring law is negative binomial,
with a dispersion that \emph{does} scale with the force of infection:
\begin{equation}
  \text{\sse{}}: \qquad
  I_t \mid \lambda_t \sim \NB\!\left(\text{mean}=R_t\lambda_t,\ \text{disp}=k\lambda_t\right).
  \label{eq:sse}
\end{equation}
That scaling is what makes $k$ an \emph{individual-level} offspring dispersion. Each infector
independently contributes $\NB(R_t w_\ell,\ k w_\ell)$ offspring at lag $\ell$; these all share
the success probability $k/(k+R_t)$, which does not depend on $\ell$, so by negative binomial
closure they pool into exactly \eqref{eq:sse}, and each individual's own offspring total is
$\NB(R,k)$. The decomposition is used again in Section~\ref{sec:naive-risk}.

Equivalently, \eqref{eq:sse} has the Poisson--Gamma representation
\begin{equation}
  \tilde\lambda_t \mid \lambda_t \sim \Gam\!\left(k\lambda_t,\ k\right),
  \qquad
  I_t \mid \tilde\lambda_t \sim \Pois\!\left(R_t\tilde\lambda_t\right),
  \label{eq:sse-convenient}
\end{equation}
in which the day's transmission opportunity is inflated or deflated by a fresh Gamma factor.
The marginal form \eqref{eq:sse} is used directly for \sse{}; the mixture representation is
needed to define its onset-anchored counterpart below.

\paragraph{Individual heterogeneity: \ssi{}.} Heterogeneity is attached to people rather
than to occasions. Each infected individual carries a latent infectivity drawn independently from
$\Gam(k,k)$, with mean $1$ and variance $1/k$, so the aggregate infectivity of the day-$t$ cohort
is $\Gam(kI_t,k)$ and infections arise as a Poisson process driven by past infectivity:
\begin{equation}
  \text{\ssi{}}: \qquad
  Y_t \mid I_t \sim \Gam\!\left(kI_t,\ k\right),
  \qquad
  I_t \mid (Y_u)_{u<t} \sim \Pois\!\left(R_t\lambda^{Y}_t\right),
  \qquad
  \lambda^{Y}_t = \sum_{s\ge1} w_s Y_{t-s}.
  \label{eq:ssi}
\end{equation}
Here $I_t = 0$ forces $Y_t = 0$. Marginally each individual's offspring total is $\NB(R,k)$, the
same as under \sse{} --- the two models are calibrated to the same individual-level offspring
distribution and differ only in mechanism. But the $Y_t$ cannot be integrated out of the joint
law and must be inferred with the other model parameters.

\paragraph{Day-level heterogeneity: \dlo{}.} Unlike the three families above, \dlo{} has no
onset-anchored counterpart in this study. The negative binomial is applied directly to the day's
aggregate incidence, with the same dispersion on every day:
\begin{equation}
  \text{\dlo{}}: \qquad
  I_t \mid \lambda_t \sim \NB\!\left(\text{mean}=R_t\lambda_t,\ \text{disp}=k\right).
  \label{eq:dlo}
\end{equation}
Its excess variance is a property of the day and does not scale with the number of infectious
people. Thus $k$ describes day-to-day variation in aggregate incidence rather than inequality in
individual transmission, and the same numerical value has a different meaning here than in
\sse{} or \ssi{}.

\begin{remark}[The Poisson limit]
\label{rmk:naive-limit}
As $k\to\infty$ the negative binomial in \eqref{eq:sse} converges to the Poisson and the Gamma
noise in \eqref{eq:ssi} degenerates to $Y_t = I_t$, so \sse{} and \ssi{} both collapse onto
\cori{}. \dlo{} loses its excess variance in the same limit, but without the parameter ever
having had an individual-level reading to lose.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Onset-anchored models}
\label{sec:onset-models}

Transmission is now referenced to the infector's own symptom onset rather than to their
unobserved infection time, which is appropriate for a disease with negligible pre-symptomatic
transmission. Two things change. The serial interval is replaced by the TOST distribution, so the
force of infection is a weighted sum of past \emph{onset} cohorts; and infection and onset become
distinct events, separated by the incubation period. Each of the three models below is the exact
onset-anchored counterpart of one of \eqref{eq:cori}, \eqref{eq:sse} and \eqref{eq:ssi}, and is
presented in the same order. \dlo{} is omitted because no onset-anchored day-level model is
compared.

\paragraph{From transmission to observations.} Let $J_t$ denote the number of infections on day
$t$ and $D_t=C_t$ the number of observed onsets. In all three models, day-$u$ infections are
carried forward to their onset days by independent incubation draws,
\begin{equation}
  (N_{u,a})_{a\ge1} \mid J_u \sim \mathrm{Multinomial}\bigl(J_u,\ (\finc_a)\bigr),
  \qquad
  D_t = \sum_{a\ge1} N_{t-a,a},
  \label{eq:incubation-split}
\end{equation}
and only the onsets are observed. The infection counts $J_t$ and allocations $N_{u,a}$ can be
integrated out exactly.

\begin{proposition}[Onsets alone suffice]
\label{prop:equivalence}
Suppose that, conditional on a non-negative day-$t$ rate $E_t$ that is a function of
$D_{0:t}$ and of any latent variables attached to days $u \le t$, the infections satisfy
$J_t \mid E_t \sim \Pois(E_t)$, and that they are allocated to onset days by
\eqref{eq:incubation-split}. Then that model and
\begin{equation}
  D_t \mid (E_u)_{u<t} \sim \Pois\!\left(\sum_{a\ge1}\finc_a E_{t-a}\right)
  \label{eq:onset-step}
\end{equation}
define the same joint law for the observed onset process $(D_t)$.
\end{proposition}

\noindent
The proof is in Appendix~\ref{app:equivalence}. It rests on Poisson thinning, which turns the
multinomial split \eqref{eq:incubation-split} into independent Poisson parts, and on Poisson
superposition, which recombines them into \eqref{eq:onset-step}; the same thinning argument
supplies the incubation pipeline of Section~\ref{sec:onset-risk}. The hypothesis covers all three
models: two have Poisson offspring directly, and \sseso{}'s negative binomial is Poisson
conditional on its Gamma factor, as in \eqref{eq:sse-convenient}. We therefore give both the
infection process and the equivalent likelihood for observed onsets.

\paragraph{Poisson baseline: \coriso{}.} A case with onset on day $u$
infects others $s$ days later with probability $\ftost_s$, in $\Pois(R_{u+s}\ftost_s)$ numbers
and independently across lags and individuals. Summing over infectors,
\begin{equation}
  \text{\coriso{}}: \qquad
  J_t \mid D_{0:t} \sim \Pois\!\left(R_t\lambda_t\right),
  \qquad
  \lambda_t = \sum_{s\ge0}\ftost_s D_{t-s},
  \label{eq:coriso-natural}
\end{equation}
together with \eqref{eq:incubation-split}. Proposition~\ref{prop:equivalence} applies with
$E_t = R_t\lambda_t$ and reduces this to
\begin{equation}
  D_t \mid D_{0:t-1} \sim
  \Pois\!\left(\sum_{a\ge1}\finc_a R_{t-a}\sum_{s\ge0}\ftost_s D_{t-a-s}\right),
  \label{eq:coriso-convenient}
\end{equation}
a deterministic recursion in the observed onsets.

\paragraph{Transmission-event heterogeneity: \sseso{}.} The offspring law is negative
binomial with a dispersion scaling with the force of infection, as in \eqref{eq:sse}:
\begin{equation}
  \text{\sseso{}}: \qquad
  J_t \mid D_{0:t} \sim \NB\!\left(\text{mean}=R_t\lambda_t,\ \text{disp}=k\lambda_t\right),
  \qquad
  \lambda_t = \sum_{s\ge0}\ftost_s D_{t-s},
  \label{eq:sseso-natural}
\end{equation}
together with \eqref{eq:incubation-split}. Writing the negative binomial as a Poisson--Gamma
mixture puts a fresh Gamma factor on each day's transmission opportunity, and
Proposition~\ref{prop:equivalence} then applies with $E_t = R_t\tilde\lambda_t$:
\begin{equation}
  \tilde\lambda_t \mid \lambda_t \sim \Gam\!\left(k\lambda_t,\ k\right),
  \qquad
  D_t \mid (\tilde\lambda_u)_{u<t} \sim
  \Pois\!\left(\sum_{a\ge1}\finc_a R_{t-a}\tilde\lambda_{t-a}\right).
  \label{eq:sseso-convenient}
\end{equation}
Here, unlike in \sse{}, the Gamma factor cannot be integrated out again: each $D_t$ pools
contributions from many days, each carrying its own independent $\tilde\lambda$, so the onsets
are \emph{not} marginally negative binomial. The day-specific Gamma effects must therefore be
retained in the likelihood.

\paragraph{Individual heterogeneity: \ssiso{}.} Each case carries a latent
infectivity drawn from $\Gam(k,k)$, so the day-$t$ onset cohort has aggregate infectivity
$\Gam(kD_t,k)$ and drives a Poisson infection process, as in \eqref{eq:ssi}:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
  \text{\ssiso{}}: \qquad
  &Y_t \mid D_t \sim \Gam\!\left(kD_t,\ k\right),
  \qquad
  J_t \mid (Y_u)_{u\le t} \sim \Pois\!\left(R_t\lambda^{Y}_t\right), \\
  &\lambda^{Y}_t = \sum_{s\ge0}\ftost_s Y_{t-s},
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:ssiso-natural}
\end{equation}
together with \eqref{eq:incubation-split}. Proposition~\ref{prop:equivalence} applies with
$E_t = R_t\lambda^{Y}_t$ and gives
\begin{equation}
  Y_t \mid D_t \sim \Gam\!\left(kD_t,\ k\right),
  \qquad
  D_t \mid (Y_u)_{u<t} \sim
  \Pois\!\left(\sum_{a\ge1}\finc_a R_{t-a}\sum_{s\ge0}\ftost_s Y_{t-a-s}\right).
  \label{eq:ssiso-convenient}
\end{equation}
The latents are the same cohort infectivities as under \ssi{} and, as there, cannot be integrated
out.

\begin{remark}[The Poisson limit]
\label{rmk:onset-limit}
As in Remark~\ref{rmk:naive-limit}, $k\to\infty$ collapses \sseso{} and \ssiso{} onto \coriso{}.
Neither \cori{} nor \coriso{} is one of the five compared models. They provide the Poisson limits
against which the overdispersed models are interpreted; \cori{} also links the analysis to a
published \'Equateur risk curve.
\end{remark}

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{When does $R$ switch?}{When does R switch?}}
\label{sec:switch}

$R$ switches from $\Rpre$ to $\Rpost$ on day~\resultnum{data.ertarrivalday}
(\resultnum{data.ertarrivaldate}) \textbf{in each model's own time index}. In the onset-anchored
models this is unambiguous: $R_t$ multiplies the TOST-weighted force of infection producing
\emph{infections} on day $t$, so the intervention acts on transmission from that date and the
resulting onsets appear an incubation period later. In the infection-anchored models $R_t$
multiplies the force of infection producing \emph{cases appearing} on day $t$, so the switch
attributes the intervention's effect to onsets from that date.

The displacement between the two conventions is the infectee's \textbf{incubation period}, not
the serial interval (Fig.~\ref{fig:schematic}D). A case with onset on day $t$ was infected on day $t - A$ with
$A \sim \finc$; the infection-anchored model dates the intervention's effect on that
transmission to $t$, the onset-anchored model to $t - A$. The relevant delay is therefore
$\mathbb{E}[A] = \resultnum{delays.incubation.mean}$\,d. (The serial interval,
\resultnum{delays.serialinterval.mean}\,d, is the infector-onset to infectee-onset gap and does
not separate the two conventions.)

This asymmetry is a consequence of onset-as-infection conflation: it is one route by which
infection-anchored models mis-date an intervention's effect. Section~\ref{sec:results-onset}
quantifies its consequences for $\Rpost$ and the risk curve.

\section{Risks of additional cases, additional transmission and sustained transmission}

\subsection{Definition}
\label{sec:estimand}

The estimand is a real-time one, and we state it as a procedure.

\begin{quote}
Fit $(\Rpre,\Rpost,k)$ \emph{and all latent states} to the observed record through day $t$
alone. Then discard the realised trajectory after day $t$, reset $R$ to $\Rreset = \Rpre$ from
day $t+1$ onwards, and simulate a counterfactual future. $\RAC(t)$ is the posterior probability
that this replicated future contains at least one further case.
\end{quote}

So $\Pr(\cdot \mid \text{data up to day } t)$ is the correct reading, and every conditioning day
carries its own fit: the curve over days $1$--$\resultnum{data.lastday}$ is
\resultnum{data.lastday} fits per model. Day $0$ is an initial condition in every model and
contributes no likelihood term, so the curve begins at day $1$.
Section~\ref{sec:smoothing} explains why the parameters cannot be estimated once and reused.

One consequence is visible at the left-hand end of every risk curve below. In the opening days
the record is a single case and a short run of silence, the reproduction number is
prior-dominated, and the risk of at least one further case is therefore well below one; it
climbs towards one as cases accumulate, and only then begins the descent the analysis is about.
A retrospective estimand cannot show this, because parameters informed by the whole outbreak
place the early risk at one by construction. The day-to-day roughness over those first weeks is
equally a feature of the estimand rather than of the sampler: each day is a separate posterior
conditioned on a separate record.

Under the onset-anchored models a second quantity becomes distinct:

\begin{itemize}
  \item $\RAC(t)$, the probability of at least one further \emph{case}, i.e.\ a further entry in
    the onset series after day $t$. It counts individuals who are already infected on day $t$ and
    become symptomatic afterwards, even if no new transmission occurs, so it does not approach
    zero until the incubation pipeline has drained. This is the directly observable quantity and
    the headline throughout.
  \item $\RAT(t)$, the probability of at least one further \emph{transmission event} after day
    $t$. It isolates the effect of relaxing interventions but is not directly observable.
  \item $\RST(t)$, the probability that the counterfactual transmission process never becomes
    extinct. ``Sustained'' means eventual non-extinction, not reaching an arbitrary finite case
    threshold.
\end{itemize}

\noindent
Under the three infection-anchored models an infection and the case it produces fall on the same
day by construction, so the two coincide \emph{by assumption} --- which is precisely the
conflation under study. $\RST$ is defined for the four models with individual offspring families
and for their Poisson limits. It is not defined for \dlo{}, whose day-level negative-binomial
variation does not identify an individual's descendants.

$\RAC(t)$ is one number per day, not a distribution: it is a posterior probability, so the
average over draws happens inside it. In each case below we obtain a conditional
zero-probability given one posterior draw, and report
\begin{equation}
\RAC(t) = 1 - \frac{1}{S}\sum_{m=1}^{S}\exp\bigl(\log \Pr(\text{no further case} \mid
  \theta^{(m)})\bigr),
  \label{eq:posterior-average}
\end{equation}
over $S$ posterior draws, and likewise for $\RAT$ and $\RST$. Because this is an average it carries
Monte-Carlo error, which is estimated from the between-chain spread and reported beside every
curve.

\subsection{Infection-anchored models}
\label{sec:naive-risk}

Fix one posterior draw. In the infection-anchored models, the observed incidence and, for
\ssi{}, the retained infectivities completely specify the reset state: there is no separate
incubation pipeline.

\paragraph{Retained transmission weight.} Define
\begin{equation}
  \Lambda(t) = \sum_{u\le t} I_u\,(1-F_{t-u})
  \quad (\text{\cori{}},\ \text{\sse{}},\ \text{\dlo{}}),
  \qquad
  \Lambda_Y(t) = \sum_{u\le t} Y_u\,(1-F_{t-u})
  \quad (\text{\ssi{}}),
  \label{eq:pooled-weight}
\end{equation}
where $(1-F_{t-u})$ is the probability that a case first appearing on day $u$ still has
transmission to come after day $t$; with $F_0 = 0$, the $u=t$ term enters with full weight.

\paragraph{Extinction under the reset.} Let $q$ be the smallest extinction fixed point of the
offspring distribution of one case after the reset:
\begin{equation}
  q = \left(1+\frac{\Rreset}{k}(1-q)\right)^{-k}
  \quad (\text{negative binomial}),
  \qquad
  q = \exp\{-\Rreset(1-q)\}
  \quad (\text{Poisson}).
  \label{eq:extinction-root}
\end{equation}
At and below criticality, $\Rreset\leq1$, the smallest fixed point is $q=1$ exactly.

\paragraph{Future-event exponent.} Let $H_{\mathrm{inf}}(t)$ be minus the log-probability that the
retained state produces no future event:
\begin{equation}
  H_{\mathrm{inf}}(t) =
  \begin{cases}
    \Rreset\Lambda(t) & (\text{\cori{}}),\\
    k\Lambda(t)\log(1+\Rreset/k) & (\text{\sse{}}),\\
    \Rreset\Lambda_Y(t) & (\text{\ssi{}}),\\
    k\displaystyle\sum_{j>t}\log(1+\Rreset\mu_j/k) & (\text{\dlo{}}),
  \end{cases}
  \qquad
  \mu_j = \sum_{s\ge1}w_s I_{j-s},
  \label{eq:infection-exponent}
\end{equation}
where the future counts in $\mu_j$ are set to zero, $I_u:=0$ for $u>t$.

\begin{proposition}[Conditional zero-probabilities, infection-anchored models]
\label{prop:naive}
Conditional on one draw of the parameters and, for \ssi{}, the retained latent infectivities,
\begin{align}
  \log\Pr(\text{no further case after }t) &= -H_{\mathrm{inf}}(t),
  \label{eq:infection-no-case}\\
  \log\Pr(\text{no further transmission after }t) &= -H_{\mathrm{inf}}(t).
  \label{eq:infection-no-transmission}\\
  \log\Pr(\text{no sustained transmission after }t)
  &=
  \begin{cases}
    \Lambda(t)\log q & (\text{\sse{}}),\\
    -\Rreset(1-q)\Lambda_Y(t) & (\text{\ssi{}}),\\
    -\Rreset(1-q)\Lambda(t) & (\text{\cori{}}).
  \end{cases}
  \label{eq:infection-no-sustained}
\end{align}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
\emph{Retained cohorts.}
For \cori{}, each observed case contributes $\Pois(\Rreset w_\ell)$ offspring at lag $\ell$
independently; the contributions with $\ell > t-u$ are untouched by the conditioning and pool by
Poisson superposition into $\Pois(\Rreset\Lambda(t))$. For \sse{}, the same contributions are
$\NB(\Rreset w_\ell,\ k w_\ell)$ and pool by negative binomial closure into
$\NB(\Rreset\Lambda(t),\ k\Lambda(t))$, whose zero-probability is as stated. For \ssi{}, given
the latents they are again Poisson, with rates $\Rreset w_\ell Y_u$, pooling into
$\Pois(\Rreset\Lambda_Y(t))$. For \dlo{}, the future counts are negative binomial day by day with
a dispersion that does not depend on the force of infection, so the zero-probabilities multiply
over days. Taking minus the logarithm gives the four rows of \eqref{eq:infection-exponent}.

\emph{Cases versus transmission.}
Because infection and symptom onset are identified in these models, a future transmission event
and the future case it creates are represented as occurring together.

\emph{Eventual extinction.}
Condition on the number $N$ of future infections seeded by the retained state. Each begins an
independent family that becomes extinct with probability $q$, so the required probability is
$\mathbb{E}[q^N]$. Under \sse{}, $N$ is
$\NB(\Rreset\Lambda(t),k\Lambda(t))$ and its pgf at $q$ is
$(1+\Rreset(1-q)/k)^{-k\Lambda(t)}=q^{\Lambda(t)}$ by
\eqref{eq:extinction-root}. Under \ssi{} it is conditionally
$\Pois(\Rreset\Lambda_Y(t))$, and under \cori{} it is
$\Pois(\Rreset\Lambda(t))$; their pgfs give the remaining rows of
\eqref{eq:infection-no-sustained}. \dlo{} has no row because it defines aggregate incidence
one day at a time rather than individual offspring families.
\end{proof}

\noindent
Thus $\RAC(t)=\RAT(t)=1-e^{-H_{\mathrm{inf}}(t)}$, while $\RST$ follows
\eqref{eq:infection-no-sustained}. The \cori{} row is included as the Poisson
reference, not as a compared model. It is the $k\to\infty$ limit of the \sse{} and \ssi{} rows,
with $\Lambda_Y(t)\to\Lambda(t)$. The \dlo{} sum is finite because $\mu_j=0$ once $j$ exceeds
$t$ plus the support of $w$.

\subsection{Onset-anchored models}
\label{sec:onset-risk}

Fix one posterior draw. Here the observed onsets do not completely specify the reset state: in
addition to retained onset cohorts, it contains a latent pipeline of individuals already infected
but not yet symptomatic. Write $R_u$ for the historical reproduction number on day $u$.

\paragraph{Retained transmission weight.} The driving series still able to transmit after $t$,
\begin{equation}
  W(t) = \sum_{u \le t} \mathcal{D}_u\, \stost_{t-u},
  \qquad
  \mathcal{D}_u =
  \begin{cases}
    D_u & (\text{\coriso}),\\
    D_u & (\text{\sseso}),\\
    Y_u & (\text{\ssiso}),
  \end{cases}
  \label{eq:retained-weight}
\end{equation}
the onset-anchored analogue of $\Lambda(t)$, with the TOST survival function of
\eqref{eq:survivals} in place of $1-F$.

\paragraph{Incubation pipeline mean.} The expected number infected on or before day $t$ whose
onset falls after it,
\begin{equation}
  M(t) = \sum_{u \le t} E_u\, \sinc_{t-u},
  \label{eq:pipeline}
\end{equation}
with $E_u$ the model's conditional mean number of infections on day $u$ --- $R_u\lambda_u$ for
\coriso{}, $R_u\tilde\lambda_u$ for \sseso{} and $R_u\lambda^{Y}_u$ for \ssiso{}, as in
\eqref{eq:coriso-convenient}--\eqref{eq:ssiso-convenient} ---
evaluated at the \emph{historical} $R_u$: the pipeline was filled before the reset.

\paragraph{Future-event exponent.} Let $c$ be minus the log-probability that one future case,
transmitting under $\Rreset$ over its whole TOST support, has no offspring:
\begin{equation}
  c =
  \begin{cases}
    \Rreset & (\text{\coriso}),\\
    k\log\!\left(1+\Rreset/k\right) & (\text{\sseso}),\\
    k\log\!\left(1+\Rreset/k\right) & (\text{\ssiso}).
  \end{cases}
  \label{eq:per-case}
\end{equation}
The exponent for no transmission from onset cohorts already observed by day $t$ is
\begin{equation}
  H_{\mathrm{on}}(t) =
  \begin{cases}
    \Rreset\,W(t) & (\text{\coriso}),\\
    c\,W(t) & (\text{\sseso}),\\
    \Rreset\,W(t) & (\text{\ssiso}).
  \end{cases}
  \label{eq:retained-exponent}
\end{equation}
For \ssiso{}, $H_{\mathrm{on}}(t)$ conditions on the inferred infectivities of onset cohorts
already observed, whereas $c$ averages over the as-yet-unobserved infectivity of a future
pipeline member.

\begin{proposition}[Conditional zero-probabilities, onset-anchored models]
\label{prop:onset}
Conditional on one draw of the parameters and the retained latent state,
\begin{align}
  \log\Pr(\text{no further case after } t) &= -H_{\mathrm{on}}(t) - M(t),
  \label{eq:log-no-case}\\
  \log\Pr(\text{no further transmission after } t)
    &= -H_{\mathrm{on}}(t) - M(t)\bigl(1 - e^{-c}\bigr).
  \label{eq:log-no-transmission}\\
  \log\Pr(\text{no sustained transmission after } t)
    &=
    \begin{cases}
      W(t)\log q-M(t)(1-q) & (\text{\sseso{}}),\\
      -\Rreset(1-q)W(t)-M(t)(1-q) & (\text{\ssiso{}},\ \text{\coriso{}}).
    \end{cases}
  \label{eq:log-no-sustained}
\end{align}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Two independent sources of future events must be accounted for: the onset cohorts retained
through day $t$, which have transmission opportunity left, and the pipeline of individuals
already infected but not yet symptomatic.

\emph{Retained cohorts.} Under \coriso{} and \ssiso{} the infections generated by cohort $u$ at
TOST lag $s$ are independent $\Pois(\Rreset \ftost_s \mathcal{D}_u)$; those with $s > t-u$ are
untouched by the conditioning, and summing over $s$ and $u \le t$ gives
$\Pois(\Rreset W(t))$, with zero-probability $e^{-\Rreset W(t)}$. Under \sseso{} the same
contributions are $\NB(\Rreset\ftost_s D_u,\ k\ftost_s D_u)$ with common success probability
$k/(k+\Rreset)$, so they pool into $\NB(\Rreset W(t),\ kW(t))$, whose zero-probability is
$(1+\Rreset/k)^{-kW(t)} = e^{-cW(t)}$. Either way the probability is
$e^{-H_{\mathrm{on}}(t)}$.

\emph{Pipeline.} Poisson splitting of the day-$u$ infections by the incubation delay (the
argument of Appendix~\ref{app:equivalence}) makes the number infected on or before $t$ but
symptomatic after $t$ Poisson with mean $M(t)$, independently of the retained cohorts' remaining
transmission.

\emph{Cases versus transmission.}
A future \emph{case} requires either a pipeline member to become symptomatic or a retained
cohort to generate a new infection --- and every new infection produces an onset strictly later,
since $\finc$ has no mass at lag~$0$. So no further case has probability
$e^{-M(t)}\,e^{-H_{\mathrm{on}}(t)}$, giving \eqref{eq:log-no-case}.

A future \emph{transmission event} permits pipeline members to become symptomatic provided each
transmits zero times, which each does independently with probability $e^{-c}$ by
\eqref{eq:per-case}. If the pipeline size is $N \sim \Pois(M(t))$ then
$\mathbb{E}\bigl[(e^{-c})^{N}\bigr] = \exp\bigl(-M(t)(1-e^{-c})\bigr)$ by the Poisson generating
function, and multiplying by $e^{-H_{\mathrm{on}}(t)}$ gives
\eqref{eq:log-no-transmission}.

For no sustained transmission, every future infection must instead start a family that
eventually becomes extinct. The retained \sseso{} cohorts seed a negative-binomial number of
infections whose pgf at $q$ is $q^{W(t)}$ by \eqref{eq:extinction-root}; the retained \ssiso{}
and \coriso{} cohorts seed a conditionally Poisson number, giving
$\exp\{-\Rreset(1-q)W(t)\}$. The pipeline contains $N\sim\Pois(M(t))$ cases and contributes
$\mathbb{E}[q^N]=\exp\{-M(t)(1-q)\}$ in every model. Multiplication gives
\eqref{eq:log-no-sustained}.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{Comparison of the two anchorings.} Both $H_{\mathrm{inf}}(t)$ and
$H_{\mathrm{on}}(t)$ describe transmission still owed by cohorts observed through day $t$.
The onset-anchored calculation adds $M(t)$, the infections already generated under the historical
$R_u$ whose onsets lie after $t$. A further \emph{case} requires that pipeline to be empty; no
further \emph{transmission} requires only that its members have no offspring
(Fig.~\ref{fig:schematic}B, C). Setting $M(t)=0$
reduces the onset-anchored expressions to the same retained-cohort structure as their
infection-anchored counterparts, with the TOST survival replacing the serial-interval survival.

\begin{remark}[Immediate consequences]
Five special cases follow immediately and are used as verification targets
(Section~\ref{sec:verification}).
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
  \item Since $1 - e^{-c} \le 1$, \eqref{eq:log-no-transmission} is never below
    \eqref{eq:log-no-case}, so $\RAC(t) \ge \RAT(t)$ always, with equality exactly when
    $M(t) = 0$. The gap is the pipeline, and it is the term the infection-anchored models set to
    zero by assumption.
  \item A sustained family requires a transmission event, so
    $\RST(t)\leq\RAT(t)\leq\RAC(t)$. Equivalently, the one-case extinction probability $q$ is at
    least its zero-offspring probability $e^{-c}$, and the same ordering follows term by term in
    \eqref{eq:log-no-transmission} and \eqref{eq:log-no-sustained}.
  \item Setting $\Rreset = 0$ gives $c = 0$ and $H_{\mathrm{on}}(t) = 0$, so $\RAT(t) = 0$
    exactly and $\RAC(t) = 1 - e^{-M(t)}$: with no future transmission permitted, the only
    remaining risk is that the retained pipeline is non-empty.
  \item As $k \to \infty$, $c = k\log(1+\Rreset/k) \to \Rreset$ and \sseso{}'s
    $H_{\mathrm{on}}(t)$ tends to
    $\Rreset W(t)$, so both overdispersed models collapse onto \coriso{}.
  \item If $\Rreset\leq1$, then $q=1$ and $\RST(t)=0$ exactly, whatever reset state remains.
\end{enumerate}
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Why time delays do not change eventual extinction]
The serial-interval and TOST distributions are normalised: they allocate a case's offspring
across future times without changing its total offspring pgf. They therefore change
$\Lambda(t)$, $W(t)$ and $M(t)$, and hence the timing of the decline in $\RST$, but not the
fixed point $q$ under a constant reset reproduction number.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Why historical and reset reproduction numbers both appear]
$H_{\mathrm{on}}(t)$ uses the reset $\Rreset$, because it describes transmission that has yet to
happen;
$M(t)$ uses the historical $R_u$, because the pipeline it counts was generated before the reset.
Under the infection-anchored models there is no pipeline, so the whole retained state is projected
at $\Rreset$. This is the formal counterpart of the timing argument of
Section~\ref{sec:results-onset}.
\end{remark}

\subsection{What the three mechanisms do differently}
\label{sec:structure}

Proposition~\ref{prop:naive} makes the structural comparison explicit. \sse{} and \ssi{} depend
on the observed history \emph{only through the scalar} $\Lambda(t)$ or $\Lambda_Y(t)$: the total
remaining offspring pools into a single negative binomial or, given the latents, a single
Poisson. \dlo{} does not. Its risk depends on the whole temporal \emph{profile}
$(\mu_j)_{j>t}$ of the future force of infection, because a fresh dispersion that does not scale
with that force is applied on each future day. Writing
$\varphi(\mu) = -k\log(1+\Rreset\mu/k)$, which is convex with $\varphi(0) = 0$ and hence
superadditive,
\begin{equation}
  -\Rreset\Lambda(t) \;\le\; \log \Pr(\text{no further case})_{\mdlo}
  = \sum_{j>t}\varphi(\mu_j)
  \;\le\; \varphi\bigl(\Lambda(t)\bigr).
  \label{eq:dlo-bounds}
\end{equation}
Spreading the same total $\Lambda(t)$ across days \emph{dilutes} the overdispersion, so a value
of $k$ calibrated as an individual-level offspring dispersion does far less work in \dlo{} than
in \sse{} or \ssi{}. The lower bound is the negative of the \cori{} exponent in
\eqref{eq:infection-exponent}, so
\dlo{}'s risk is squeezed towards \cori{}'s precisely when the future force of infection is thin
and spread out.

\begin{remark}
The bounds \eqref{eq:dlo-bounds} hold always; the \emph{direction} of the comparison with \sse{}
does not. It is a property of a thin, spread-out profile rather than a theorem, and concentrating
$\Lambda(t)$ into a day or two reverses it.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Why every conditioning day carries its own fit}
\label{sec:smoothing}

Real-time conditioning applies to the parameters as well as to the latent states, and the two
cannot be separated. Under \ssi{}, \sseso{} and \ssiso{} the reproduction numbers and the latent
infectivities are correlated in the posterior: a history is explained either by a higher $R$ or
by more infectious cohorts, and the data through day $t$ constrain that trade-off jointly.
Retaining a latent state conditioned on the first $t$ days beside parameters conditioned on the
whole record is therefore not the day-$t$ posterior of either, and refitting is what the estimand
requires rather than a refinement of it.

The alternative is nonetheless informative, and we report it as a comparison rather than adopting
it: one fit to the complete record, with the latent state re-derived at each day by a bootstrap
particle filter run at every parameter draw. \dlo{} and \sse{} have no latent state, so for them
that comparison isolates the effect of conditioning the parameters alone, which makes them a
useful control when reading it.

Only the conditioning changes. Because $\Lambda(t)$, $W(t)$ and $M(t)$ sum over days $u\le t$,
truncating the record to day $t$ leaves the risk formulas of Propositions~\ref{prop:naive}
and~\ref{prop:onset} untouched.

\subsection{Integrating out the remaining latent states}
\label{sec:reconstruction}

Some latent states are integrated out of the likelihood analytically
(Section~\ref{sec:latent-block}) but still enter the reset state --- in particular infections
after the final observed onset, which may remain in the incubation pipeline. They need not be
recovered individually. In every model the conditional log zero-probability is \emph{affine} in
the latent trajectory,
\begin{equation}
\log \Pr(\text{no further event after } t \mid \theta, Y)
  = -\Bigl(\alpha(\theta) + \sum_u \beta_u(\theta)\, Y_u\Bigr),
  \qquad \beta_u \ge 0,
  \label{eq:affine-risk}
\end{equation}
and the removed states are conditionally independent $\Gam(A_u, B_u)$ given $\theta$, with
$A_u = k\,\mathrm{scale}_u$ and $B_u = k + c_u$. The Gamma moment generating function therefore
integrates them out of the risk exactly,
\begin{equation}
\mathbb{E}\bigl[e^{-\beta_u Y_u} \mid \theta\bigr] = (1 + \beta_u / B_u)^{-A_u},
  \label{eq:latent-mgf}
\end{equation}
so no Monte-Carlo reconstruction step is involved and the risk carries no sampling error beyond
that of the posterior draws themselves. This is a third exact marginalisation, composing with the
two already performed: the likelihood integrates these states out of the observation density, and
\eqref{eq:latent-mgf} integrates the same states out of the risk.
For $\RST$, the retained-infectivity coefficient is $\Rreset(1-q)$ and every historical
incubation-pipeline coefficient is multiplied by $(1-q)$; the existing $\RAC$ and $\RAT$
coefficients are unchanged.

\section{Inference}
\label{sec:inference}
% =============================================================================================

\subsection{Priors}
\label{sec:priors}

We use proper priors, shared across models within each analysis, so that marginal likelihoods
and posterior model probabilities are well defined.

\begin{itemize}
  \item $\Rpre$ and $\Rpost$: independent log-normals with median
    \resultnum{priors.R.median} and 95\% interval
    $(\resultnum{priors.R.quantilelower},\ \resultnum{priors.R.quantileupper})$.
  \item $k$, where estimated: a log-normal centred on the literature value and a decade wide on
    each side of it, with median \resultnum{priors.k.median} and 95\% interval
    $(\resultnum{priors.k.quantilelower},\ \resultnum{priors.k.quantileupper})$, identical in
    every model. The width is deliberate: under a prior only a factor of two wide on each side,
    a posterior that reproduced its prior could not be told apart from one the data had
    confirmed.
  \item Prior model probabilities: uniform over the models in each analysis, i.e.\
    $1/\resultnum{naivemodelsfixedk.nmodels}$ in Analyses~1--2 and
    $1/\resultnum{onsetmodelsfixedk.nmodels}$ in Analyses~3--4.
\end{itemize}

\begin{remark}[Identical priors are necessary but not sufficient for a fair comparison]
Identical priors do not imply identical interpretations. In \sse{} and \ssi{}, $k$ is an
individual-level offspring dispersion; in \dlo{}, it controls day-level incidence variation
(Section~\ref{sec:structure}). Analysis~1 applies an individual-level literature estimate to all
three models to test its portability. Its posterior model probabilities therefore compare the
full model specifications, including that imposed value, and should not be read as evidence about
mechanism alone.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Posterior sampling}
\label{sec:sampling}

All fits use PyMC, with \resultnum{naivemodelsfixedk.chains} chains of
\resultnum{naivemodelsfixedk.draws} draws after \resultnum{naivemodelsfixedk.tune} tuning
iterations in every analysis. Fits with fixed $k$, and estimated-$k$ fits without an inverse-CDF
latent block, use Hamiltonian Monte Carlo throughout (the No-U-Turn sampler, NUTS). In
estimated-$k$ fits of \ssi{}, \sseso{} and \ssiso{}, the Gamma quantile is differentiable with
respect to its probability coordinate but not its shape. PyMC therefore uses a compound kernel:
univariate Slice sampling for $k$, and NUTS for $\Rpre$, $\Rpost$ and the latent Uniform
coordinates. This is the same compound sampler evaluated by the parameterisation benchmark. The
NUTS target acceptance probability is \resultnum{naivemodelsfixedk.targetaccept} in
Analyses~1--3 and \resultnum{onsetmodelsestimatedk.targetaccept} in Analysis~4. Each fit jointly
estimates $\Rpre$, $\Rpost$, any latent states, and --- in Analyses~2 and~4 --- $k$. Because the
estimand conditions on the record through the day in question (Section~\ref{sec:smoothing}), each
model is fitted once per conditioning day, and the diagnostics below cover all of those fits. The
evidence and dispersion comparisons are properties of a model given the whole record, and use the
fit to the complete series, which also serves as the final conditioning day.

Under incomplete reporting, the free count block is the number unreported on each day. Its
scalar support makes PyMC assign its ordinary coordinate-wise Metropolis kernel; the continuous
parameters and Gamma inverse-CDF coordinates retain their samplers above.

\paragraph{Convergence.} The four analyses rest on
\resultnum{core.diagnostics.fits} fits in total. Across every one of them, and over every
sampled variable including the latent blocks, the worst $\hat{R}$ is
\resultnum{core.diagnostics.maxrhat}, the smallest bulk effective sample size is
\resultnum{core.diagnostics.minbulkess}, and the total number of divergent NUTS transitions is
\resultnum{core.diagnostics.divergences}. Per analysis the minimum bulk effective sample sizes
are \resultnum{naivemodelsfixedk.diagnostics.minbulkess},
\resultnum{naivemodelsestimatedk.diagnostics.minbulkess},
\resultnum{onsetmodelsfixedk.diagnostics.minbulkess} and
\resultnum{onsetmodelsestimatedk.diagnostics.minbulkess}; the smallest of them, and the worst
$\hat{R}$, both come from Analysis~4, which is the hardest geometry in the study and the reason
its sampler runs at $\text{target\_accept} =
\resultnum{onsetmodelsestimatedk.targetaccept}$ (Section~\ref{sec:priors}). The three variants
of Section~\ref{sec:results-variants} add \resultnum{variants.diagnostics.fits} fits, of which
\resultnum{variants.diagnostics.sampledfits} sample anything at all --- under
\resultnum{noswitchfixedr.fixedrpre}-fixed $R$ and $k$, \sse{} has no free variable and its
fits are point masses --- with a worst $\hat{R}$ of
\resultnum{variants.diagnostics.maxrhat} over those that do. The analysis workflow refuses to
produce a risk curve if any of the fits behind it exceeds $\hat{R} =
\resultnum{convergence.maxrhat}$ or shows divergent
transitions in more than 1\% of draws, so these are enforced criteria rather than a report of
what happened to occur.

\subsection{Latent-state sampling}
\label{sec:latent-block}

The Gamma latent variables, particularly in \sseso{}, include very small shape parameters. We
combine exact marginalisation with an inverse-CDF parameterisation to obtain stable posterior
sampling without changing the model.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Exact marginalisation.} A latent that reaches no observation day carrying a case
    enters the likelihood only through an exponential factor linear in that latent. Its Gamma
    prior is conjugate to this factor, giving
    $\int \Gam(y;\,k\,\mathrm{scale},\,k)\,e^{-cy}\,\mathrm{d}y =
    (1+c/k)^{-k\,\mathrm{scale}}$ and a conditional posterior
    $\Gam(k\,\mathrm{scale},\ k+c)$. This removes
    \resultnum{onsetmodelsfixedk.sseso.latents.marginalised} of \sseso{}'s
    \resultnum{onsetmodelsfixedk.sseso.latents.total} latents, leaving
    \resultnum{onsetmodelsfixedk.sseso.latents.sampled} sampled, and removes
    \resultnum{onsetmodelsfixedk.ssi.latents.marginalised} of \ssi{}'s and \ssiso{}'s
    \resultnum{onsetmodelsfixedk.ssi.latents.total} on the complete record, and more on the
    shorter records of earlier conditioning days. Section~\ref{sec:reconstruction} shows how they
    re-enter the risk calculation exactly.
  \item \textbf{Inverse-CDF reparameterisation.} The surviving latents are sampled as
    $\mathrm{Uniform}(0,1)$ variates transformed through the corresponding Gamma quantile
    function.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Model evidence}
\label{sec:evidence}

We compute the marginal likelihood
$p(D_{1:\resultnum{data.lastday}} \mid \text{model})$ by integrating over both parameters
and latent states. Bridge sampling is the primary estimator; prior Monte Carlo and importance
sampling provide independent numerical checks, and deterministic quadrature is used where the
parameter space is sufficiently small. Posterior model probabilities follow from these marginal
likelihoods and the uniform model prior of Section~\ref{sec:priors}.

\subsection{Comparing dispersion posteriors}
\label{sec:dispersion}

Where $k$ is estimated, we compare every pair of posterior distributions using three
complementary quantities: the \emph{median ratio}, which measures location; the \emph{overlap}
$\int\min(p_a,p_b)$, a scale-free symmetric measure; and $\Pr(k_a > k_b)$ for independent draws.
All are computed on the log scale, as the density panels are drawn; the overlap is invariant to
that choice because both densities acquire the same Jacobian.

\subsection{Model and numerical validation}
\label{sec:verification}

We validated the models against analytic limits and independent simulation. A deterministic
one-day incubation period recovers the infection-anchored models after shifting the time index;
as $k\to\infty$, \sse{} and \ssi{} converge to \cori{}, while \sseso{} and \ssiso{} converge to
\coriso{}. Simulations from the explicit infection-and-incubation process agree with the
onset-only representation of Proposition~\ref{prop:equivalence}. Explicit reset simulations also
agree with the risk formulas in Propositions~\ref{prop:naive} and~\ref{prop:onset}, including the
$\Rreset=0$ limit in which $\RAT=0$ and $\RAC$ is the probability that the incubation pipeline is
non-empty.
The extinction roots are checked against their offspring generating functions, including the
negative-binomial-to-Poisson limit, and the RST formulas are compared with independent
Galton--Watson continuation simulation for all four branching models and both Poisson limits.

Particle filtering provides an independent check of the latent-state calculations, and particle
marginal Metropolis--Hastings reproduces the parameter posteriors on synthetic data. Comparisons
against particle methods match the conditioning on both sides: at fixed parameters the closed
forms are held against a particle \emph{smoother}, while the filtering states target the
comparison estimator of Section~\ref{sec:smoothing}. The affine representation
\eqref{eq:affine-risk} is verified against the risk formulas by the identity
$\beta_u = \log \Pr(\cdot \mid Y = 0) - \log \Pr(\cdot \mid Y = e_u)$, which linearity makes
exact, and the resulting marginalisation \eqref{eq:latent-mgf} is checked against averaging
explicit draws from the same conditional distribution. Bridge-sampling estimates agree with
deterministic quadrature for
the low-dimensional models and with alternative estimators for the latent models. Finally, in
the Poisson limit the analysis recovers the published reproduction-number posterior and
\'Equateur risk curve of Thompson et al.\ (2024), after accounting for the one-day difference in
time-indexing convention.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}
% =============================================================================================

\noindent
Four analyses are reported. Analyses~1 and~2 compare the three infection-anchored models, with
$k$ held at the literature value \resultnum{naivemodelsfixedk.fixedk} and then estimated;
Analyses~3 and~4 add the onset-anchored models to \sse{} and \ssi{}, again fixed and then

Section~\ref{sec:results-variants} and the supplement. Throughout, ``the crossing'' means the first day on which a curve falls below a
threshold \emph{and stays there}. Values quoted at day~\resultnum{data.referenceday}
(\resultnum{data.referencedate}) are read from the fitted curves partway down their descent,
where they are furthest apart.

\subsection{Mechanism, at a transplanted dispersion parameter}
\label{sec:results-naive-fixed}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{../figures/naive_models_fixed_k/naive_models_fixed_k.pdf}
  \caption{\textbf{The mechanism of overdispersion changes the end-of-outbreak risk, at a
    dispersion parameter whose interpretation differs across models.} \dlo{}, \sse{}
    and \ssi{} fitted to the \'Equateur onset series with $k$ fixed at
    \resultnum{naivemodelsfixedk.fixedk}. \textbf{A}, \textbf{B}: posterior densities for
    $\Rpre$ and $\Rpost$, over the shared prior. \textbf{C}: posterior model probabilities under
    a uniform prior over the \resultnum{naivemodelsfixedk.nmodels} models. Because the fixed
    value of $k$ has a different interpretation in \dlo{}, this panel compares the complete model
    specifications rather than mechanism alone. \textbf{D}: risk of additional cases against
    conditioning day, with daily
    onsets behind it, the ERT arrival and withdrawal marked, and the $0.05$ and $0.01$ decision
    thresholds drawn. The marker on each curve is its $0.05$ crossing.}
  \label{fig:naive-fixed}
\end{figure}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{The same numerical $k$ produces little overdispersion in \dlo{}.} At
    day~\resultnum{data.referenceday} the risk is
    \resultnum{naivemodelsfixedk.dlo.rac.reference} under \dlo{} against
    \resultnum{naivemodelsfixedk.sse.rac.reference} under \sse{} and
    \resultnum{naivemodelsfixedk.ssi.rac.reference} under \ssi{} --- a factor of more than four
    between models handed an identical dispersion parameter. This is the dilution bound
    \eqref{eq:dlo-bounds} in practice: the future force of infection after the last case is thin
    and spread over the whole serial interval, which is exactly the regime in which a day-level
    $k$ does almost nothing and \dlo{} is pressed against its Poisson lower bound.
  \item \textbf{The consequence for a declaration is weeks, not days.} \dlo{}'s risk first
    settles below $0.05$ on day~\resultnum{naivemodelsfixedk.dlo.rac05.day}
    (\resultnum{naivemodelsfixedk.dlo.rac05.date}), against
    day~\resultnum{naivemodelsfixedk.sse.rac05.day} for \sse{} and
    day~\resultnum{naivemodelsfixedk.ssi.rac05.day} for \ssi{}. At the $0.01$ threshold the
    ordering holds and the spread is wider still: \dlo{} settles on
    day~\resultnum{naivemodelsfixedk.dlo.rac01.day}
    (\resultnum{naivemodelsfixedk.dlo.rac01.date}), against
    day~\resultnum{naivemodelsfixedk.sse.rac01.day} for \sse{} and
    day~\resultnum{naivemodelsfixedk.ssi.rac01.day} for \ssi{}. Both of \dlo{}'s crossings fall after the
    ERT actually withdrew: on that day it still places
    \resultnum{naivemodelsfixedk.dlo.rac.withdrawal} on a further case, against
    \resultnum{naivemodelsfixedk.sse.rac.withdrawal} and
    \resultnum{naivemodelsfixedk.ssi.rac.withdrawal}.
  \item \textbf{\ssi{} lies on the same side of \sse{} as \dlo{} does.} After the final case
    \ssi{}'s risk is \emph{above} \sse{}'s
    (\resultnum{naivemodelsfixedk.ssi.rac.reference} against
    \resultnum{naivemodelsfixedk.sse.rac.reference} at day~\resultnum{data.referenceday}), which
    reads backwards until one asks what each model's heterogeneity is attached to. \ssi{}'s is a
    property of \emph{people who have been observed}: \resultnum{data.totalcases} known
    individuals have now gone weeks without infecting anyone, which is direct evidence they were
    not the superspreading kind, and $\Lambda_Y(t)$ is revised down accordingly. \sse{}'s is a
    property of \emph{events that have not happened yet}, about which a run of zeros says
    nothing, so \sse{} gets no such revision --- its entire retained state is $\Lambda(t)$. At
    matched expected further cases, \sse{}'s residual risk is a rare burst and \ssi{}'s a more
    likely singleton, and the question ``any further case?'' sees the second as the larger risk.
    The contrast is therefore not \dlo{} against the other mechanisms: it is how many units the
    same $\Lambda(t)$ is divided over and whether each unit's dispersion survives to time $t$.
    \dlo{} divides over
    future days, \ssi{} over past individuals, \sse{} not at all.
  \item \textbf{The ordering is a property of the question as much as of the models.} Asking
    instead whether transmission would re-establish reverses \sse{} and \ssi{} on these same
    data, because a cluster seeds a new chain far more readily than a lone case. Both orderings
    are correct answers to different questions, and the end-of-outbreak criterion in use asks the
    first.
  \item \textbf{The model probabilities strongly favour \ssi{}} ---
    \resultnum{naivemodelsfixedk.ssi.probability} against
    \resultnum{naivemodelsfixedk.dlo.probability} for \dlo{} and
    \resultnum{naivemodelsfixedk.sse.probability} for \sse{}, from log evidences of
    \resultnum{naivemodelsfixedk.ssi.logevidence}, \resultnum{naivemodelsfixedk.dlo.logevidence}
    and \resultnum{naivemodelsfixedk.sse.logevidence} nats. At a fixed $k$ this partly reports
    that \resultnum{naivemodelsfixedk.fixedk} is a better value for \ssi{} than for the other
    two, which is the transplantation error restated rather than independent evidence about
    mechanism. Analysis~2 separates the two readings.
  \item \textbf{$\Rpost$ is comparable across the three models} ---
    \resultnum{naivemodelsfixedk.dlo.rpost.interval},
    \resultnum{naivemodelsfixedk.sse.rpost.interval} and
    \resultnum{naivemodelsfixedk.ssi.rpost.interval} (posterior median and 95\% credible
    interval) --- so the divergence in the risk curves does not come from a different inferred
    effect of the intervention. The corresponding $\Rpre$ estimates are
    \resultnum{naivemodelsfixedk.dlo.rpre.interval},
    \resultnum{naivemodelsfixedk.sse.rpre.interval} and
    \resultnum{naivemodelsfixedk.ssi.rpre.interval}.
  \item \textbf{Monte-Carlo error does not account for the separation.} Over the case-free tail
    in which the crossings occur, the largest Monte-Carlo standard error on any of the three
    curves is \resultnum{naivemodelsfixedk.ssi.rac.mcselate}, two orders of magnitude below the
    differences above.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Mechanism, with the dispersion estimated}
\label{sec:results-naive-estimated}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{../figures/naive_models_estimated_k/naive_models_estimated_k.pdf}
  \caption{\textbf{The models infer different dispersion parameters, and their risk curves
    remain separated.} As Fig.~\ref{fig:naive-fixed}, with
    $k$ estimated. \textbf{C}: the three $k$ posteriors over the prior they share, with each
    model's posterior median and 95\% credible interval in the legend. \textbf{D}: posterior
    model probabilities. \textbf{E}: risk of additional cases, with the view starting at the
    ERT's arrival because from there until well past the last case all three curves sit at $1$.
    The curves are computed over the whole window either way; only the view is trimmed.}
  \label{fig:naive-estimated}
\end{figure}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{The main split in $k$ is between individual- and day-level heterogeneity.} The
    posterior medians (95\% credible intervals) are
    \resultnum{naivemodelsestimatedk.dlo.k.interval} for \dlo{},
    \resultnum{naivemodelsestimatedk.sse.k.interval} for \sse{} and
    \resultnum{naivemodelsestimatedk.ssi.k.interval} for \ssi{}. \dlo{} against \ssi{}: overlap
    \resultnum{naivemodelsestimatedk.dlo-vs-ssi.overlap}, median ratio
    \resultnum{naivemodelsestimatedk.dlo-vs-ssi.medianratio},
    $\Pr(k_{\mdlo}>k_{\mssi})=\resultnum{naivemodelsestimatedk.dlo-vs-ssi.probabilitygreater}$.
    \sse{} against \ssi{}: overlap \resultnum{naivemodelsestimatedk.sse-vs-ssi.overlap}, ratio
    \resultnum{naivemodelsestimatedk.sse-vs-ssi.medianratio}, probability
    \resultnum{naivemodelsestimatedk.sse-vs-ssi.probabilitygreater}. But \dlo{} against \sse{}:
    overlap \resultnum{naivemodelsestimatedk.dlo-vs-sse.overlap} and
    $\Pr(k_{\mdlo}>k_{\msse})=\resultnum{naivemodelsestimatedk.dlo-vs-sse.probabilitygreater}$ ---
    the two are barely distinguishable. \textbf{It is \ssi{} that is the outlier, not \dlo{}.}
  \item \textbf{The split is day-level against individual-level, which is where
    onset-as-infection bites hardest.} \dlo{} and \sse{} both attach their excess variance to a
    \emph{day}: \dlo{} to the day's aggregate incidence, \sse{} to the day's pooled transmission
    through a freshly drawn $\lambda_t$. \ssi{} attaches it to the \emph{individual}. The series
    being fitted is onsets, and an incubation period of mean \resultnum{delays.incubation.mean}\,d
    and standard deviation \resultnum{delays.incubation.sd}\,d scatters each day's infections
    forward over a wide kernel --- so a model reading onsets as infections sees day-to-day
    variation that the convolution has already largely averaged out, and answers with a larger
    $k$ (less overdispersion) than the infection process carried. \ssi{} is close to invariant to
    this, because the convolution \emph{regroups} individuals across days while the aggregate
    infectivity of $n$ independent individuals is $\Gam(kn,k)$ whichever $n$ they are: regrouping
    moves the cohorts, not the variance. Because all three models here are infection-anchored,
    this analysis alone cannot separate that explanation from the structural difference of
    Section~\ref{sec:structure}; the onset-anchored comparison provides that test.
  \item \textbf{Only \ssi{}'s posterior is compatible with the literature value.} \ssi{}'s 95\%
    credible interval, $(\resultnum{naivemodelsestimatedk.ssi.k.lower},
    \resultnum{naivemodelsestimatedk.ssi.k.upper})$, contains
    \resultnum{naivemodelsfixedk.fixedk}; \dlo{}'s
    $(\resultnum{naivemodelsestimatedk.dlo.k.lower},
    \resultnum{naivemodelsestimatedk.dlo.k.upper})$ and \sse{}'s
    $(\resultnum{naivemodelsestimatedk.sse.k.lower},
    \resultnum{naivemodelsestimatedk.sse.k.upper})$ do not: both sit an order of magnitude above
    it, and \sse{}'s median exceeds even the 97.5th percentile of its own prior,
    \resultnum{priors.k.quantileupper}. The prior is not what puts them there --- it is a decade
    wide on each side of \resultnum{priors.k.median} (Section~\ref{sec:priors}), so these
    posteriors are the data's answer and not the prior's.
  \item \textbf{The evidence says the same thing more sharply.} Letting $k$ move is worth
    \resultnum{naivemodelsestimatedk.sse.evidencegain} nats to \sse{} and
    \resultnum{naivemodelsestimatedk.dlo.evidencegain} to \dlo{}, but only
    \resultnum{naivemodelsestimatedk.ssi.evidencegain} to \ssi{}. Each gain is net of what the
    wide prior costs in the marginal likelihood --- a model that gains by being released has to
    pay for the room it was given --- so Analysis~1 was charging \dlo{} and \sse{} for a value
    poorly supported under those models, whereas \resultnum{naivemodelsfixedk.fixedk} is
    compatible with \ssi{} on this series.
  \item \textbf{The risk gap persists when $k$ is estimated.} At each model's inferred $k$,
    \dlo{} still crosses $0.05$ last, on
    day~\resultnum{naivemodelsestimatedk.dlo.rac05.day}, against
    day~\resultnum{naivemodelsestimatedk.sse.rac05.day} for \sse{} and
    day~\resultnum{naivemodelsestimatedk.ssi.rac05.day} for \ssi{}; at $0.01$ the three fall on
    days \resultnum{naivemodelsestimatedk.dlo.rac01.day},
    \resultnum{naivemodelsestimatedk.sse.rac01.day} and
    \resultnum{naivemodelsestimatedk.ssi.rac01.day}. Estimating $k$ does not close the gap, it
    redraws it: \ssi{}, whose $k$ falls furthest, now settles earliest at both thresholds, and
    \sse{}, whose $k$ rises furthest, moves back towards \dlo{} and the Poisson limit.
    Misapplying a literature $k$ creates the gap; the day-level mechanism sustains it.
  \item \textbf{The posterior model probabilities keep their direction} --- \ssi{}
    \resultnum{naivemodelsestimatedk.ssi.probability}, \dlo{}
    \resultnum{naivemodelsestimatedk.dlo.probability}, \sse{}
    \resultnum{naivemodelsestimatedk.sse.probability}. Because each model has its own inferred
    dispersion, this comparison is not confounded by imposing the same numerical value of $k$.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Onset anchoring}
\label{sec:results-onset}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Onset anchoring advances both crossings by a week or more.} At fixed $k$
    (Fig.~\ref{fig:onset-fixed}), \sseso{} crosses $0.05$ on
    day~\resultnum{onsetmodelsfixedk.sseso.rac05.day} against \sse{}'s
    day~\resultnum{onsetmodelsfixedk.sse.rac05.day}, an advance of
    \resultnum{onsetmodelsfixedk.sseso-vs-sse.shift05} days, and $0.01$ on
    day~\resultnum{onsetmodelsfixedk.sseso.rac01.day} against
    day~\resultnum{onsetmodelsfixedk.sse.rac01.day}, an advance of
    \resultnum{onsetmodelsfixedk.sseso-vs-sse.shift01} days. \ssiso{} advances \ssi{}'s
    crossings by \resultnum{onsetmodelsfixedk.ssiso-vs-ssi.shift05} and
    \resultnum{onsetmodelsfixedk.ssiso-vs-ssi.shift01} days respectively
    (day~\resultnum{onsetmodelsfixedk.ssiso.rac05.day} against
    day~\resultnum{onsetmodelsfixedk.ssi.rac05.day}, and
    day~\resultnum{onsetmodelsfixedk.ssiso.rac01.day} against
    day~\resultnum{onsetmodelsfixedk.ssi.rac01.day}). The displacement is on the scale of the
    mean incubation period, \resultnum{delays.incubation.mean}\,d.
  \item \textbf{Why the direction is \emph{earlier}, and why it is not a shorter generation
    time.} All four models share the same onset-to-onset serial interval; what changes is which
    side of the conditioning day a transmission falls on once that interval is cut into
    transmission and incubation pieces. Proposition~\ref{prop:onset} makes this explicit: the
    onset-anchored models place infection at transmission time, so infections already generated
    under the historical $\Rpost$ enter $M(t)$ and stay in the incubation pipeline when control is
    reset, while the remaining TOST opportunity $W(t)$ of older onset cohorts is already largely
    spent. The infection-anchored models have no pipeline, move the infection decision to the
    later symptom-onset date, and apply the reset $\Rreset$ to it, which makes the residual state
    look younger and drain later.
  \item \textbf{The infection-anchored convention more than doubles the inferred $\Rpost$.} The
    posterior medians (95\% credible intervals) are
    \resultnum{onsetmodelsfixedk.sse.rpost.interval} for \sse{} and
    \resultnum{onsetmodelsfixedk.ssi.rpost.interval} for \ssi{}, against
    \resultnum{onsetmodelsfixedk.sseso.rpost.interval} for \sseso{} and
    \resultnum{onsetmodelsfixedk.ssiso.rpost.interval} for \ssiso{}. The infection-anchored
    convention applies the day-\resultnum{data.ertarrivalday} switch to \emph{onsets}, so
    post-arrival onsets that arose from pre-arrival infections are charged to the controlled
    period. $\Rpre$ is far more stable across the four ---
    \resultnum{onsetmodelsfixedk.sse.rpre.interval},
    \resultnum{onsetmodelsfixedk.ssi.rpre.interval},
    \resultnum{onsetmodelsfixedk.sseso.rpre.interval} and
    \resultnum{onsetmodelsfixedk.ssiso.rpre.interval} --- consistent with an effect on
    \emph{when} the switch lands rather than on the epidemic's early growth.
  \item \textbf{The onset-anchored models are strongly preferred, and are indistinguishable from
    each other.} The posterior model probabilities are
    \resultnum{onsetmodelsfixedk.sseso.probability} for \sseso{} and
    \resultnum{onsetmodelsfixedk.ssiso.probability} for \ssiso{}, against
    \resultnum{onsetmodelsfixedk.ssi.probability} for \ssi{} and
    \resultnum{onsetmodelsfixedk.sse.probability} for \sse{}; the log evidences are
    \resultnum{onsetmodelsfixedk.sseso.logevidence},
    \resultnum{onsetmodelsfixedk.ssiso.logevidence},
    \resultnum{onsetmodelsfixedk.ssi.logevidence} and
    \resultnum{onsetmodelsfixedk.sse.logevidence} nats.
  \item \textbf{Estimating $k$ preserves the ordering but makes the advance mechanism-dependent}
    (Fig.~\ref{fig:onset-estimated}). The onset-anchored pair still settle first and the model
    ordering is unchanged (\resultnum{onsetmodelsestimatedk.sseso.probability},
    \resultnum{onsetmodelsestimatedk.ssiso.probability},
    \resultnum{onsetmodelsestimatedk.ssi.probability},
    \resultnum{onsetmodelsestimatedk.sse.probability}), as is the $\Rpost$ contrast. What changes
    is the size of the advance: \sseso{} now crosses
    \resultnum{onsetmodelsestimatedk.sseso-vs-sse.shift05} and
    \resultnum{onsetmodelsestimatedk.sseso-vs-sse.shift01} days before \sse{}, while \ssiso{}
    leads \ssi{} by only \resultnum{onsetmodelsestimatedk.ssiso-vs-ssi.shift05} and
    \resultnum{onsetmodelsestimatedk.ssiso-vs-ssi.shift01} days, against
    \resultnum{onsetmodelsfixedk.sseso-vs-sse.shift05} and
    \resultnum{onsetmodelsfixedk.ssiso-vs-ssi.shift05} days respectively at fixed $k$. The
    asymmetry is on the infection-anchored side: releasing $k$ moves \sse{} and \ssi{} in
    opposite directions (Section~\ref{sec:results-naive-estimated}), so the gap each has to
    close differs, while the two onset-anchored curves barely move at all.
  \item \textbf{Under onset anchoring the data say almost nothing about $k$.} \sseso{}'s
    posterior is \resultnum{onsetmodelsestimatedk.sseso.k.interval} and \ssiso{}'s
    \resultnum{onsetmodelsestimatedk.ssiso.k.interval}, against a prior with median
    \resultnum{priors.k.median} and 95\% interval
    \resultnum{priors.k.quantilelower}--\resultnum{priors.k.quantileupper}: the posteriors sit on
    the prior's median and retain most of its width, and releasing $k$ is worth
    \resultnum{onsetmodelsestimatedk.sseso.evidencegain} and
    \resultnum{onsetmodelsestimatedk.ssiso.evidencegain} nats --- nothing, to within the
    estimation error. \textbf{This is what the prior's width is for.} A posterior reproducing an
    informative prior is ambiguous: it looks the same whether the data agree with the prior or
    carry no information at all. Here the prior spans a decade on each side of
    \resultnum{priors.k.median} (Section~\ref{sec:priors}), and the posterior still reproduces
    it, which the second reading alone explains. Under onset anchoring the dispersion sits on a
    latent transmission process an incubation period removed from the observed onsets, and
    \resultnum{data.totalcases} cases do not locate it.
  \item \textbf{That makes the attenuation prediction untestable on the onset-anchored side of
    this series.} The prediction is that a model reading onsets as infections should infer a
    \emph{larger} $k$ than one that represents incubation explicitly, because the convolution has
    already averaged out day-to-day variation. \sse{}'s posterior,
    \resultnum{onsetmodelsestimatedk.sse.k.interval}, does sit an order of magnitude above
    \sseso{}'s --- median ratio \resultnum{onsetmodelsestimatedk.sse-vs-sseso.medianratio},
    overlap \resultnum{onsetmodelsestimatedk.sse-vs-sseso.overlap},
    $\Pr(k_{\msse}>k_{\msseso}) =
     \resultnum{onsetmodelsestimatedk.sse-vs-sseso.probabilitygreater}$ --- but by the bullet
    above that comparison holds an identified posterior against a prior, so it cannot be read as
    attenuation measured. The \ssi{}/\ssiso{} pair overlap
    \resultnum{onsetmodelsestimatedk.ssi-vs-ssiso.overlap} and are not separated at all. What
    survives as evidence for the mechanism is Analysis~2's split between day-level and
    individual-level dispersion (Section~\ref{sec:results-naive-estimated}), where every
    posterior compared is identified by the data.
  \item \textbf{The advance is specific to this analysis.} Its direction is conditional on the
    reset and switch conventions, the outbreak history and the posterior; it is not a general
    result for arbitrary time series or interventions.
\end{itemize}

    \resultnum{onsetmodelsfixedk.ssiso.rat05.day}. On $\RST$ the four settle on
    days~\resultnum{onsetmodelsfixedk.sse.rst05.day} (\sse{}),
    \resultnum{onsetmodelsfixedk.sseso.rst05.day} (\sseso{}),

    pipeline cross the conditioning day, but not the eventual-extinction root under the constant
    reset reproduction number.
  \item \textbf{Sustained transmission changes the ranking of the two heterogeneity mechanisms.}
    At the reference day, \sse{} has $\RST=
    \resultnum{onsetmodelsfixedk.sse.rst.reference}$ against
    \resultnum{onsetmodelsfixedk.ssi.rst.reference} for \ssi{}, even though their $\RAC$ values
    are \resultnum{onsetmodelsfixedk.sse.rac.reference} and
    \resultnum{onsetmodelsfixedk.ssi.rac.reference}, respectively. \sse{} assigns the remaining
    risk to rarer, larger bursts: a burst is less likely to occur, but more likely than an isolated
    transmission to start a lineage that survives. Thus the ordering measured for $\RAC$ is not an
    ordering of late-outbreak risk in general; it depends on the event being asked about. The
    anchoring comparison, by contrast, points the same way on all three.
  \item \textbf{Only onset anchoring separates cases from transmission, by about a quarter of a
    unit of probability at the widest.} $\RAC$ exceeds $\RAT$ by at most
    \resultnum{onsetmodelsfixedk.sseso.gap.max} for \sseso{}, on
    day~\resultnum{onsetmodelsfixedk.sseso.gap.day}
    (\resultnum{onsetmodelsfixedk.sseso.gap.date}), and by at most
    \resultnum{onsetmodelsfixedk.ssiso.gap.max} for \ssiso{}, on
    day~\resultnum{onsetmodelsfixedk.ssiso.gap.day}. The gap peaks shortly after the last
    observed onset (day~\resultnum{data.lastonsetday}), when the pipeline is largest relative to
    the transmission still to come, and closes as it drains.
  \item \textbf{It delays a case-based declaration by most of a week.} \sseso{}'s $\RAT$ settles
    below $0.05$ on day~\resultnum{onsetmodelsfixedk.sseso.rat05.day} and below $0.01$ on
    day~\resultnum{onsetmodelsfixedk.sseso.rat01.day}, against
    day~\resultnum{onsetmodelsfixedk.sseso.rac05.day} and
    day~\resultnum{onsetmodelsfixedk.sseso.rac01.day} for $\RAC$ --- later by
    \resultnum{onsetmodelsfixedk.sseso.gap.shift05} and
    \resultnum{onsetmodelsfixedk.sseso.gap.shift01} days. For \ssiso{} the same comparison
    gives \resultnum{onsetmodelsfixedk.ssiso.gap.shift05} and
    \resultnum{onsetmodelsfixedk.ssiso.gap.shift01} days ($\RAT$ on days
    \resultnum{onsetmodelsfixedk.ssiso.rat05.day} and
    \resultnum{onsetmodelsfixedk.ssiso.rat01.day} against $\RAC$ on days
    \resultnum{onsetmodelsfixedk.ssiso.rac05.day} and
    \resultnum{onsetmodelsfixedk.ssiso.rac01.day}).
  \item \textbf{The two quantities answer different questions, and the difference is
    decision-relevant.} $\RAT$ isolates what relaxing interventions does; $\RAC$ is what a
    surveillance system would actually see. A criterion written on cases keeps interventions in
    place while the pipeline drains, even though transmission risk has already settled --- and an
    infection-anchored model, which identifies the two by assumption, cannot represent that
    distinction at all.
  \item \textbf{Estimating $k$ changes very little here} (Fig.~\ref{fig:sustained-estimated}): the
    widest gap between $\RAC$ and $\RAT$ is \resultnum{onsetmodelsestimatedk.sseso.gap.max} for
    \sseso{} and \resultnum{onsetmodelsestimatedk.ssiso.gap.max} for \ssiso{}, against
    \resultnum{onsetmodelsfixedk.sseso.gap.max} and
    \resultnum{onsetmodelsfixedk.ssiso.gap.max} at fixed $k$ --- a consequence of the
    onset-anchored $k$ posteriors sitting on the prior's median, which is the literature value.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Incomplete reporting}
\label{sec:results-underreporting}

Every result above reads the observed onset series as the true one. A day with no reported onset
is then a day with no onset, and it is exactly those days that drive the risk down. Relaxing the
assumption asks what a case-free record is worth when cases can go unseen.

The observation model is the one of Section~\ref{sec:estimand} applied to true onsets
$D_t=c_t+U_t$, where $U_t$ is the latent unreported count. For a Poisson count law the exact
split is $c_t\sim\operatorname{Pois}(\pi\mu_t)$ and
$U_t\sim\operatorname{Pois}((1-\pi)\mu_t)$. For a negative-binomial
count law, the reported marginal and the conditional law of $U_t$ are negative binomial as well.
This is algebraically identical to $D_t$ followed by $c_t\mid D_t\sim\Binom(D_t,\pi)$, without
requiring a potential term. The estimand is unchanged and still
concerns \emph{true} cases: $\RAC(t)$ is the posterior probability of at least one further true
onset, whether or not anyone would record it. Only $D_0=c_0$ is fixed, so no day after the first
is constrained to have been fully observed. $\pi$ is an assumed input, not an estimate --- these
data cannot identify it without external information --- so Fig.~\ref{fig:underreporting} is a
sweep over assumed values rather than a fit.

The reporting sweeps were regenerated with this unreported-count parameterisation. All fits pass
the configured convergence gate and the onset-anchored results below are unchanged from the
algebraically equivalent totals-plus-binomial run. The infection-anchored models were added to
the sweep only afterwards, so they have no counterpart under the earlier parameterisation and
are not part of that comparison. No threshold crossing moved by more than one
day and the pointwise differences have no systematic direction. Agreement is not uniformly
within the chain-based combined Monte-Carlo standard error, however, so that stricter numerical
comparison remains a publication check rather than being treated as established by the matching
convergence summaries alone.

    \resultnum{onsetmodelsfixedk.sseso.gap.shift05} days under complete reporting,
    \resultnum{underreporting80.sseso.gap.shift05} at

    \resultnum{underreporting60.sseso.gap.shift05} at

    \resultnum{underreporting60.ssiso.gap.shift05}. The reporting assumption moves the two curves

    \resultnum{underreporting80.reportingpercent}\% curves lie above the completely-reported one

    adding a hidden case raises risk. These curves are separately refitted, however, so their
    parameter and latent posteriors also change; monotone ordering across assumed reporting
    probabilities is therefore an empirical result here, not a theorem or a correctness
    requirement.
  \item \textbf{The delay is on the order of a week, and it grows as the assumption is
    weakened.} \sseso{} first falls below 0.05 on
    \resultnum{onsetmodelsfixedk.sseso.rac05.date} under complete reporting,
    \resultnum{underreporting80.sseso.rac05.date} at
    \resultnum{underreporting80.reportingpercent}\% reporting, and

    \resultnum{underreporting60.reportingpercent}\%. For \ssiso{} the same three dates are

    \resultnum{underreporting80.sse.rac05.date} and \resultnum{underreporting60.sse.rac05.date},

    \resultnum{underreporting80.ssi.rac05.date}. At
    \resultnum{underreporting60.reportingpercent}\% reporting \ssi{} is the last to cross,

    (\resultnum{underreporting60.ssi.rac05.date}) --- the very day the ERT withdrew, where it
    stands at \resultnum{underreporting60.ssi.rac.withdrawal}. That is close enough to the line,
    against a late-window Monte-Carlo error of
    \resultnum{underreporting60.ssi.rac.mcselate}, that the crossing day itself is uncertain by

  \item \textbf{At the stricter threshold, every crossing falls after the ERT withdrew.} At
    0.01, \sseso{} settles on day~\resultnum{onsetmodelsfixedk.sseso.rac01.day} under complete
    reporting, day~\resultnum{underreporting80.sseso.rac01.day} at
    \resultnum{underreporting80.reportingpercent}\% and
    day~\resultnum{underreporting60.sseso.rac01.day} at
    \resultnum{underreporting60.reportingpercent}\%; \ssiso{} behaves the same way
    (days \resultnum{onsetmodelsfixedk.ssiso.rac01.day},
    \resultnum{underreporting80.ssiso.rac01.day} and
    \resultnum{underreporting60.ssiso.rac01.day}). The infection-anchored models are later
    again: \sse{} moves from day~\resultnum{onsetmodelsfixedk.sse.rac01.day} to
    \resultnum{underreporting80.sse.rac01.day} and
    \resultnum{underreporting60.sse.rac01.day}, and \ssi{} from

    \resultnum{underreporting80.ssi.rac01.day} and

    day~\resultnum{data.withdrawalday}, the day the response actually ended, by between a
    fortnight and two. A declaration criterion written at 0.01 is therefore not robust to a
    reporting assumption this weak on a record of this length --- and note that locating these
    crossings at all requires the window to run past the withdrawal
    (Section~\ref{sec:outbreak}); ended there, the run could only have reported that four
    curves were still above the threshold.

    choice between the two superspreading mechanisms is the smallest: \sse{} and \ssi{} cross
    0.05 on days \resultnum{underreporting80.sse.rac05.day} and
    \resultnum{underreporting80.ssi.rac05.day} at
    \resultnum{underreporting80.reportingpercent}\% reporting, and the onset-anchored pair are

    \resultnum{underreporting60.reportingpercent}\% is worth a comparable amount --- \sseso{}

    \resultnum{onsetmodelsfixedk.sse.rac05.day} to \resultnum{underreporting60.sse.rac05.day}.

    and each matters more than which superspreading mechanism is assumed.
  \item \textbf{The cost is in the sampling, not the model.} With the true counts random, nothing in
    the latent block can be marginalised --- \sseso{} carries
    \resultnum{underreporting60.sseso.latents.sampled} sampled latents against
    \resultnum{onsetmodelsfixedk.sseso.latents.sampled} under complete reporting --- and the
    unreported counts add a discrete block of their own. Across the two reporting sweeps,
    \resultnum{reporting.diagnostics.fits} fits have a worst $\hat{R}$ of
    \resultnum{reporting.diagnostics.maxrhat}, a smallest bulk ESS of
    \resultnum{reporting.diagnostics.minbulkess}, and
    \resultnum{reporting.diagnostics.divergences} divergent transitions. At the weakest
    assumption, convergence is still adequate across all
    \resultnum{underreporting60.diagnostics.fits} fits at
    \resultnum{underreporting60.reportingpercent}\% reporting (worst $\hat{R}$
    \resultnum{underreporting60.diagnostics.maxrhat}, smallest bulk ESS
    \resultnum{underreporting60.diagnostics.minbulkess}), but the Monte-Carlo standard error on
    the late curve is correspondingly larger:
    \resultnum{underreporting60.sseso.rac.mcselate} against
    \resultnum{onsetmodelsfixedk.sseso.rac.mcselate} under complete reporting. Reaching that
    required \resultnum{underreporting60.draws} draws after
    \resultnum{underreporting60.tune} tuning steps per chain, against
    \resultnum{onsetmodelsfixedk.draws} and \resultnum{onsetmodelsfixedk.tune} for the
    completely-reported analyses: the discrete unreported-count block is the slowest-mixing part
    of the model and sets the budget.
\end{itemize}

A reporting \emph{delay} from onset to report is implemented alongside this and would
right-truncate recent days automatically, one conditioning day at a time. It is not used here:
the \'Equateur line list gives onset dates rather than reporting dates, so there is nothing to
estimate the delay from.

\subsection{What the switchpoint and superspreading contribute}
\label{sec:results-variants}

Every measurement of the naive/onset difference above is made in models that also carry
superspreading and an $R$ switchpoint dated in each model's own time index
(Section~\ref{sec:switch}). Three variants of Analysis~3 remove one of those ingredients at a
time. Each is fitted and evaluated exactly as the main analyses are --- a fit per conditioning
day, model evidence by bridge sampling --- and each has a supplementary figure.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Superspreading removed} (Fig.~\ref{fig:no-superspreading}). \cori{} and
    \coriso{}, the $k \to \infty$ Poisson limits of the two anchoring conventions, are set beside
    Analysis~3's \ssi{} and \ssiso{}, the best-supported infection- and onset-anchored models in
    the main text. The four form a two-by-two comparison: anchoring varies with the mechanism
    held fixed, and superspreading is switched on with the anchoring held fixed. \ssi{} and
    \ssiso{} are Analysis~3's own fits, and because the two analyses share the data, the $R$
    priors and the switch, their evidences and those of \cori{} and \coriso{} are normalised
    together into one set of posterior model probabilities. That asks whether superspreading is
    better supported than Poisson transmission at all.
  \item \textbf{No switchpoint, $R$ and $k$ fixed} (Fig.~\ref{fig:no-switch-fixed}). The four
    models of Analysis~3 with $R$ held at a single value throughout and $k$ at the literature
    value. Nothing is estimated but the latent states, so any naive/onset difference that
    survives is the arithmetic of the retained state alone, with both conventions handed
    identical parameters. \sse{}, which has no latent state, has no free variable at all; its
    evidence is its likelihood, computed exactly.
  \item \textbf{No switchpoint, one $R$ estimated} (Fig.~\ref{fig:no-switch-single}). As above,
    but each model infers a single reproduction number for the whole outbreak. Against the
    previous variant this adds back one thing: what the two conventions conclude $R$ was.
\end{itemize}

With the switchpoint removed, the reset to $\Rpre$ in the definition of $\RAC$ is a no-op: the
curves in Figs.~\ref{fig:no-switch-fixed} and~\ref{fig:no-switch-single} are forward posterior
predictives rather than the reset predictive of the main analyses, and are to be compared with
each other rather than with Fig.~\ref{fig:onset-fixed}.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Most of the anchoring advance is the switchpoint.} Removing it leaves \sseso{}
    only \resultnum{noswitchfixedr.sseso-vs-sse.shift05} days ahead of \sse{} at $0.05$ and
    \resultnum{noswitchfixedr.ssiso-vs-ssi.shift05} days ahead for \ssiso{} against \ssi{},
    against \resultnum{onsetmodelsfixedk.sseso-vs-sse.shift05} and
    \resultnum{onsetmodelsfixedk.ssiso-vs-ssi.shift05} days with the switch in place at the same
    fixed $k$ (Table~\ref{tab:crossings}). Estimating one $R$ per model instead of fixing it
    changes nothing here --- the four posteriors are
    \resultnum{noswitchsingler.sse.rpre.interval},
    \resultnum{noswitchsingler.ssi.rpre.interval},
    \resultnum{noswitchsingler.sseso.rpre.interval} and
    \resultnum{noswitchsingler.ssiso.rpre.interval}, indistinguishable from each other, and the
    crossings are the same days as under the fixed $R$. \textbf{So the
    \resultnum{delays.incubation.mean}-day displacement of the intervention
    (Section~\ref{sec:switch}) is what the advance mostly measures}, not the retained state: with no intervention to mis-date, the two conventions
    almost agree about when to declare.
  \item \textbf{What the retained state contributes on its own is the $\RAC$/$\RAT$ gap.} It
    survives the switchpoint's removal intact --- \resultnum{noswitchfixedr.sseso.gap.shift05}
    days for \sseso{} and \resultnum{noswitchfixedr.ssiso.gap.shift05} for \ssiso{}, against
    \resultnum{onsetmodelsfixedk.sseso.gap.shift05} and
    \resultnum{onsetmodelsfixedk.ssiso.gap.shift05} with the switch --- because the incubation
    pipeline is a property of the model rather than of the intervention. The onset-anchored
    models still hold their $\RAC$ back by about a week after $\RAT$ has settled when nothing
    about the response is being estimated at all.
  \item \textbf{Superspreading is decisively better supported than Poisson transmission under
    infection anchoring, and only weakly so under onset anchoring.} Over the four models of
    Fig.~\ref{fig:no-superspreading}, the posterior model probabilities are
    \resultnum{onsetmodelsnosuperspreading.combined.ssiso.probability} for \ssiso{},
    \resultnum{onsetmodelsnosuperspreading.combined.coriso.probability} for \coriso{},
    \resultnum{onsetmodelsnosuperspreading.combined.ssi.probability} for \ssi{} and
    \resultnum{onsetmodelsnosuperspreading.combined.cori.probability} for \cori{}. The naive
    pair are separated by nearly seven nats
    (\resultnum{onsetmodelsnosuperspreading.combined.ssi.logevidence} against
    \resultnum{onsetmodelsnosuperspreading.combined.cori.logevidence}), the onset-anchored pair
    by under one (\resultnum{onsetmodelsnosuperspreading.combined.ssiso.logevidence} against
    \resultnum{onsetmodelsnosuperspreading.combined.coriso.logevidence}). Read with
    Section~\ref{sec:results-onset}: once incubation is represented explicitly, much of what
    individual-level dispersion was accounting for is already explained, which is the same
    result the unidentified $k$ posteriors report from the other direction.
  \item \textbf{Anchoring moves the declaration further than superspreading does, and the two
    interact.} With superspreading removed, anchoring alone is worth
    \resultnum{onsetmodelsnosuperspreading.coriso-vs-cori.shift05} days at $0.05$ (\cori{}
    against \coriso{}) --- twice what it is worth in the models that carry it,
    \resultnum{onsetmodelsfixedk.ssiso-vs-ssi.shift05} days for \ssi{} against \ssiso{}.
    Superspreading, in turn, is worth
    \resultnum{onsetmodelsnosuperspreading.combined.ssi-vs-cori.shift05} days under infection
    anchoring (\cori{} against \ssi{}) but only
    \resultnum{onsetmodelsnosuperspreading.combined.ssiso-vs-coriso.shift05} under onset
    anchoring (\coriso{} against \ssiso{}). Each mechanism brings the declaration forward, and
    each does so by less in the presence of the other: both act on the same residual risk, and
    once either has removed it there is less left for the second to remove.
\end{itemize}

\section{Planned sensitivity analyses}
\label{sec:sensitivity}
% =============================================================================================

The following analyses remain to be completed before drawing conclusions about robustness.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Outbreak-specific serial interval} (mean
    \resultnum{delays.alternativeserialinterval.mean}\,d, standard deviation
    \resultnum{delays.alternativeserialinterval.sd}\,d) --- \emph{infection-anchored models
    only}. Its lighter tail may reduce the estimated risk after a long case-free period. It is
    incompatible with the onset-anchored models under the independent incubation/TOST
    decomposition because its variance is smaller than the incubation variance
    (Section~\ref{sec:serial-interval}).
  \item \textbf{Incubation/TOST decomposition.} Re-estimate \sseso{} and \ssiso{} under
    alternative published Ebola incubation distributions while holding the onset-to-onset serial
    interval fixed. This will quantify dependence on a decomposition that the serial interval
    alone does not identify.
  \item \textbf{Retrospective rather than real-time conditioning.} Retain the state at day $t$
    from a fit to the complete record, as an explicitly retrospective counterpart to the
    estimand of Section~\ref{sec:estimand}. This asks a different question --- what the evidence
    now says about a past decision, rather than what was knowable at the time --- and the gap
    between the two answers is itself the quantity of interest.
  \item \textbf{Shifted $R$-switch for the infection-anchored models.} The onset-anchoring
    comparison confounds model structure with the indexing convention for the intervention.
    Moving the switch in the infection-anchored models back by
    $\mathbb{E}[A] = \resultnum{delays.incubation.mean}$ days will help separate the effect of
    intervention indexing from the effect of model structure.
  \item \textbf{Non-empty initial incubation pipeline} for the onset-anchored models. Seed one or
    two already-infected individuals at day~\resultnum{data.firstday} with residual incubation
    drawn from $\finc$, and measure the effect on $\Rpre$ and the risk curve. This assesses
    sensitivity to whether the first observed case is the epidemiological index case
    (Section~\ref{sec:outbreak}).
  \item \textbf{Prior sensitivity}, particularly for the prior on $k$, whose interpretation
    differs between models. Its width is already a decade on each side of the literature value
    (Section~\ref{sec:priors}); its location, and the priors on $R$, are untested.
\end{enumerate}

\appendix
\section{The two forms of the onset-anchored models are equivalent}
\label{app:equivalence}
% =============================================================================================

This appendix proves Proposition~\ref{prop:equivalence} and gives the additional argument needed
for \eqref{eq:sseso-natural}. Throughout, the explicit infection-process form is as defined in
Section~\ref{sec:onset-models}: infections $J_t$ are drawn from the model's offspring
distribution, and the $J_u$ infections of day $u$ are allocated to onset days by the incubation
split \eqref{eq:incubation-split}, of which only the totals $D_t$ are observed.

\subsection*{Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:equivalence}}

By hypothesis $J_t \mid E_t \sim \Pois(E_t)$, with $E_t$ measurable after the day-$t$ onset
cohort and any associated latent state have been determined.

\emph{Step 1 --- Poisson thinning.} Multinomial splitting of a Poisson count yields
\emph{independent} Poisson parts: conditional on $E_u$, $N_{u,a}\sim\Pois(E_u\finc_a)$
independently over $a \ge 1$, with $J_u = \sum_a N_{u,a}$ recovered as their sum. The latent
$J_u$ therefore carries no information beyond the parts and can be dropped. (This is also the
step that makes the incubation pipeline of \eqref{eq:pipeline} Poisson.)

\emph{Step 2 --- Poisson superposition.} $D_t = \sum_{a\ge1}N_{t-a,a}$ is a sum of independent
Poissons, hence $D_t \mid (E_u)_{u<t} \sim \Pois\bigl(\sum_{a\ge1}\finc_a E_{t-a}\bigr)$, which
is \eqref{eq:onset-step}.

\emph{Step 3 --- the joint law, not just the marginal.} Because $\finc$ has no mass at lag~$0$,
$E_{t-a}$ for $a\ge1$ depends only on onset cohorts and associated states dated no later than
$t-1$, so the display above is precisely the
conditional law $p(D_t \mid D_{0:t-1})$. The parts $\{N_{u,a} : u+a=t\}$ composing $D_t$ are
disjoint from those composing any other $D_{t'}$, so distinct days' onsets are conditionally
independent given the $E$s. Chaining $p(D_{0:T}) = \prod_t p(D_t\mid D_{0:t-1})$ gives equality
of the full joint laws. \hfill$\square$

\subsection*{Why the hypothesis covers all three models}

\coriso{} and \ssiso{} satisfy it directly, with $E_t = R_t\lambda_t$ and
$E_t = R_t\lambda^{Y}_t$ respectively; under \ssiso{} the Gamma infectivities are explicit in
both forms and the thinning and superposition arguments do not touch them.

\sseso{} needs one further step, because its offspring law \eqref{eq:sseso-natural} is negative
binomial rather than Poisson. If $\tilde\lambda_t \mid \lambda_t \sim \Gam(k\lambda_t,k)$ and
$J_t\mid\tilde\lambda_t\sim\Pois(R_t\tilde\lambda_t)$, then
$R_t\tilde\lambda_t\sim\Gam(k\lambda_t,\ k/R_t)$ and, marginally,
$J_t\sim\NB(\text{mean}=R_t\lambda_t,\ \text{disp}=k\lambda_t)$ --- which is exactly
\eqref{eq:sseso-natural}. So \eqref{eq:sseso-convenient} merely \emph{un-marginalises} the
negative binomial into an explicit per-day Gamma random effect, after which $J_t$ is Poisson
conditional on $\tilde\lambda_t$ and the proposition applies with $E_t = R_t\tilde\lambda_t$. The
$\tilde\lambda_t$ are independent across $t$ given $(\lambda_t)$, and each $\lambda_t$ is a
function of $D_{\le t}$, so the day-$t$ conditional still depends only on $D_{0:t-1}$ once
$\tilde\lambda_{<t}$ is integrated out. \hfill$\square$

\section{Data and code availability}
\label{app:reproducibility}
% =============================================================================================

The accompanying repository contains the daily onset series, analysis configuration, source code,
posterior samples, derived quantities, figures and this manuscript. The full analysis is
reproducible from the onset data through a documented Snakemake workflow. Numerical values in the
manuscript are generated from the saved analysis outputs so that text and figures remain
synchronised. The repository also includes the simulation, particle-method and
marginal-likelihood validation analyses described in Section~\ref{sec:verification}.

Because the estimand conditions on the record through each day in question, the risk curves are
the expensive step rather than a summary of a single fit, and the workflow reflects that: each
curve is produced by one Markov chain Monte Carlo fit per conditioning day, and the sampler
diagnostics for every one of those fits are written alongside the curve. The workflow refuses to
produce a curve if any of its fits fails the convergence criteria of
Section~\ref{sec:sampling}. The comparison estimator of Section~\ref{sec:smoothing}, which fits
the complete record once and re-derives the latent states by particle filtering, is available
through the same configuration and is used only for the comparison reported there.
